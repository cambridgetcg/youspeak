# Mitákuye oyás'iŋ — the phrase as it is built and as it sounds

_The two existing Lakota files carry the cosmology and the rites. Neither takes the phrase apart, gives
its sounds, or says how YOUSPEAK's spelling of it differs from Lakota. This file does only that. Its
first duty is the one the template puts first: this is a living tradition, its words belong to its
speakers, and even the way to write them is a matter the Lakota nations are still deciding among
themselves. Its main finding for the anchor chapter is grammatical: the phrase is spoken in the first
person singular. It says "my relatives, all". It contains no "we"._

---

## 0. Protocol notice — read first

*Mitákuye oyás'iŋ* is a phrase of living Lakota prayer and ceremony. Wikipedia reports Oglala Lakota
sources describing it as "spoken at both the beginning and the end of ceremonies", citing Andrew
Ironshell as quoted by Aaron Huey in *Granta* (2014), and the Osage theologian George Tinker's
observation that, used to end prayers, it "functions somewhat like the word 'Amen' in European and
American Christianity", a comparison of function, not of meaning [verified: as reported by Wikipedia
"Mitakuye Oyasin", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitakuye_Oyasin; the Granta and *Sojourners*
originals were not opened — Granta refused the request].

**Two hazards belong to this phrase specifically.**

1. *Appropriation.* Wikipedia, citing the *Lakota Country Times*, records that the Ojibwe use of the
   phrase during a 1960s spiritual revival was criticised by some Lakota as the work of "cultural
   thieves" [verified: as reported by Wikipedia "Mitakuye Oyasin"; the archived newspaper article was
   not opened]. YOUSPEAK's use of the phrase as a liturgical anchor sits inside that same hazard.
2. *Orthography is contested.* Several spellings are in use. In 2012 the Rosebud Sioux Tribe adopted
   the orthography of Albert White Hat Sr. by Tribal Resolution No. 2012-343, which "also banned the
   Lakota Language Consortium and its 'Czech orthography' from the reservation and its educational
   system"; Standing Rock adopted the LLC's "Standard Lakota Orthography" for teaching "but not without
   resistance from members of the community"; teachers and speakers on Pine Ridge differ over
   diacritics [verified:
   Wikipedia "Lakota language", Orthographies section, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakota_language;
   the resolution itself not opened]. On 3 May 2022 the Standing Rock Sioux Tribal Council "voted nearly
   unanimously to banish the Lakota Language Consortium", with its co-founder Wilhelm Meya and its head
   linguist Jan Ullrich [verified: G. L. Brewer, NBC News, 3 June 2022,
   https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/native-american-language-preservation-rcna31396]. Ullrich is the
   author of the *New Lakota Dictionary* [from memory: not re-checked; the NBC report says only that he
   worked on "a new Lakota dictionary"]. This
   file therefore names every spelling by its source and calls none of them "the standard".

**The discipline this file keeps:**

1. *Custodians named, as authors and authorities.* Lakota speakers, elders and ceremonial leaders; the
   tribal nations and their schools, among them the Rosebud Sioux Tribe and Sinte Gleska University,
   the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe and Sitting Bull College, Red Cloud Indian School on Pine Ridge, and
   speakers at Cheyenne River [verified: all named in Wikipedia "Lakota language", Orthographies]. Among
   scholars: Ella Cara Deloria (Yankton Dakota), Albert White Hat Sr. (Sinte Gleska), Delphine Red
   Shirt (Oglala Lakota) [verified: same article].
2. *Reception, not extraction.* Custodianship of the phrase stays with the Lakota. YOUSPEAK's
   *mitakuyame* is YOUSPEAK's word and must not be presented as Lakota.
3. *Consultation desirable.* Review by Lakota speakers is desirable before *mitakuyame* or the
   *mitakuyame:* anchor is elaborated further, taught, or set in a font.
4. *Community correction is a retirement condition.* If Lakota speakers or a tribal body ask that the
   phrase not be used, or that it be used differently, the coinage and the anchor are withdrawn or
   changed accordingly, without argument.
5. *Published material only.* Everything here comes from published dictionaries and grammars, public
   encyclopaedic articles and news reporting. No recording, no restricted ceremonial knowledge and no
   unpublished community material was sought or used.

## 1. The concept in compact form

The phrase is two words.

- **mitákuye** [miˈtakujɛ] = **mi-** "my" + **takúye** "a relative". Riggs's Dakota dictionary gives the
  noun *takúye* "a relative" with the paradigm forms *mitakuye* and *uŋkitakuyepi* [verified: Riggs
  1890, *A Dakota-English Dictionary*, p. 455, https://archive.org/details/adakotaenglishd00rigggoog].
- **oyás'iŋ** [oˈjasʼɪ̃] "all". Riggs enters the Dakota *owasiŋ* "all, every one; the whole, the greater
  part" with the Teton (Lakota) form *oyasiŋ* [verified: Riggs 1890, p. 393], and separately *oyasiŋ*,
  "T. all: i.q. owasiŋ" [verified: p. 397].

Translations in print include "all my relatives", "we are all related" and "all my relations"
[verified: Wikipedia "Mitakuye Oyasin", citing Maroukis 2005: 160]. Francis White Bird, writing in the
*Lakota Country Times*, proposed "everything is related to the existence of all my Lakota relatives"
[verified: as reported by Wikipedia; original not opened].

**Structural properties (what any faithful use must preserve):**

1. **First person singular.** *mi-* is the first-person-singular possessor prefixed to nouns of
   relationship. Riggs: "The pronouns of the first and second persons prefixed to nouns signifying
   relationship are, Sing., mi, my, ni, thy; Dual, uŋki (my and thy) our; Plur., uŋki-pi, our, ni-pi,
   your" [verified: Riggs 1893, *Dakota Grammar, Texts, and Ethnography*, §22.1, p. 15,
   https://archive.org/details/dakotagrammartex00rigguoft]. The phrase therefore stands, grammatically,
   on the speaker's own side: *my* relatives.
2. **No "we" in it.** The first-plural possessive exists — Riggs's own paradigm gives *uŋkitakuyepi*
   "our relatives" [verified: Riggs 1890, p. 455; gloss from §22.1 of Riggs 1893] — and the phrase does
   not use it. "We are all related" adds a "we" the Lakota does not say.
3. **Plurality comes from "all".** *takúye* is not marked plural in the phrase; a plural form
   *mitákuyepi* "my relatives" exists [verified: Wiktionary "takúye", usage example,
   https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tak%C3%BAye; uncited there]. The phrase leaves the noun bare and lets
   *oyás'iŋ* carry the totality.
4. **Two stressed syllables, one per word.** *mi-TÁ-ku-ye*, *o-YÁ-s'iŋ* (§4.3; the division before
   ⟨s'⟩ follows from reading ⟨s'⟩ as one ejective consonant, and no source opened gives it).

**Sibling distinctions.** *mitákuye* "my relative(s)" against *uŋkitakuyepi* "our relatives": the phrase
chooses *my*. *takú* "relative" against *táku* "something": the two differ in stress in Riggs's entries,
and no source opened in this session derives one from the other, so the attractive reading "all my
relations = all things" has no support here as etymology.

## 3. Primary attestations and scholarly references

## 4. Etymology and family

### 4.1 Morphology

- **mi-** is the possessive prefix of the first person singular, used with nouns of relationship;
  *ni-* is the second person; the third person takes a suffix *-ku*, after *i* *-tku* or *-ču* (Riggs
  gives *suŋkaku* "his younger brother", *čiŋhiŋtku* "his or her son") [verified: Riggs 1893, §22,
  p. 15]. Lakota forms of the same pattern appear in modern spelling elsewhere; the pattern is Riggs's
  description of Dakota.
- **takúye** "a relative" sits beside *takú* "a relative, kindred" and the verb *takúya* "to have one
  for a relation" [verified: Riggs 1890, p. 455]. How *-ye* relates *takúye* to *takú* is not stated in
  the sources opened.
- **oyás'iŋ** is entered by Riggs as an unanalysed adjective "all", with the Dakota cognate *owasiŋ*
  [verified: Riggs 1890, pp. 393, 397]. I found no source that segments it further.

Wikipedia states that spelling "without diacritics" is common in popular texts and fails to mark stress
and several consonant contrasts [verified: Wikipedia "Lakota language", Orthographies]. The
orthography of Albert White Hat Sr., adopted by the Rosebud Sioux Tribe, was not consulted for this
phrase; how it writes the phrase is not known to this file.

### 4.3 Sound

On the conventions Wikipedia describes for the LLC orthography [verified: Wikipedia "Lakota language",
Phonology and Orthographies]:

- **Stress.** "All monomorphemic words have one vowel which carries primary stress and has a higher tone
  than all other vowels in the word. This is generally the vowel of the second syllable of the word,
  but often the first syllable can be stressed, and occasionally other syllables as well." Stress is
  written with the acute. The rule is stated for monomorphemic words, and *mitákuye* is not one
  (*mi-* + *takúye*), so the stress here is taken from the written acute, not derived from the rule.
  *mi.tá.ku.ye* and *o.yá.s'iŋ* both carry the acute on the second syllable. Riggs's *ta-ku'-ye* stresses
  the second syllable of the bare stem; with *mi-* prefixed, the written stress moves to *tá*, again
  the second syllable of the word.
- **Vowels.** Five oral vowels /i e a o u/ and three nasal vowels /ĩ ã ũ/, "phonetically [ɪ̃ ə̃ ʊ̃]";
  /e/ and /o/ are "said to be more open", nearer [ɛ] and [ɔ]. Nasal vowels are written with a following
  ⟨ŋ⟩ (or ⟨ƞ⟩, ⟨n⟩), and "no syllables end with consonantal /n/". So the final ⟨iŋ⟩ of *oyás'iŋ* is the
  single nasal vowel [ɪ̃], not [i] plus a consonant; the English respelling "oh-YAH-seen" in the
  existing dig adds an [n] that Lakota does not have.
- **The apostrophe.** The orthography uses ⟨ʼ⟩ for the glottal stop /ʔ/, and also writes the ejectives
  with an apostrophe: ⟨č' ȟ' k' p' s' š' t'⟩. Lakota has ejective fricatives, among them /sʼ/ written
  ⟨sʼ⟩. On these conventions the ⟨s'⟩ of *oyás'iŋ* is the ejective fricative [sʼ], produced with a
  glottal closure, rather than an [s] followed by a separate glottal-stop segment. Read that way, it is
  a single consonant and opens the last syllable (*o.yá.s'iŋ*). I found no source that divides the
  word as *oyás + 'iŋ*. Whether every speaker realises it as [sʼ] was not checked against recordings.
  Note that the Dakota cognate *owasiŋ* has plain *s* in Riggs.

**IPA of the phrase:** /miˈtakuje oˈjasʼĩ/, phonetically about [miˈtakujɛ oˈjasʼɪ̃].

### 4.4 How YOUSPEAK's spelling departs

YOUSPEAK's *mitakuyame* [/mi.tɑ.ku.jɑ.meɪ/, "mee-TAH-koo-yah-may", per labs/logos/experiments/core/
156-mitakuyame.md, line 183] differs from the Lakota phrase in six ways:

1. **It drops *oyás'iŋ* entirely**, and with it the nasal vowel, the ejective and the word "all".
   Experiment 156 says the *-me* suffix "carries" the "all"; that is YOUSPEAK's reassignment, not Lakota
   meaning.
2. **It changes final *-e* to *-a*.** script/codepoints.md (line 175) gives the derivation as
   "mitakuya + -me". *mitakuya* is not an attested Lakota or Dakota form in the sources opened. It
   happens to resemble *mi-* + the verb *takúya* "to have one for a relation", whose attested
   first-person forms are *takuwaya* and, with *ma-* "me", *takumayaŋ* [verified: Riggs 1890, p. 455,
   as far as the OCR is legible], so it reads, if at all, as a malformed verb rather than as "my
   relative" (my inference; no source discusses *mitakuya*).
3. **It drops the acute.** Stress is unmarked, though YOUSPEAK's respelling "mee-TAH-…" keeps the
   second-syllable stress of *mitákuye*.
4. **It adds Sumerian *-me*** as a family suffix: a cross-family compound, by design.
5. **Vowel quality.** YOUSPEAK writes [ɑ] and an English diphthong [eɪ]; Lakota has /a/, and its /e/ is
   a monophthong nearer [ɛ].
6. **Syllabification.** Experiment 156 writes "*mi-TÁK-uye*"; the Lakota syllables, following Riggs's
   *ta-ku'-ye*, are *mi-tá-ku-ye*.

None of this forbids a coinage; YOUSPEAK forms its words its own way. It means *mitakuyame* should be
described as "after *mitákuye*", not as a spelling of the Lakota.

### 4.5 Family and contact

Lakota belongs to the Mississippi Valley genus of Siouan [verified: WALS genus "Mississippi Valley
Siouan", https://wals.info/feature/39A.tab] and "is mutually intelligible with the two dialects of the
Dakota language" [verified: Wikipedia "Lakota language", lead]. That Lakota, Dakota and Nakota form one
dialect continuum is [from memory: not re-checked]. The Dakota pair
*owasiŋ* / Teton *oyasiŋ* shows the dialect relation within one word [verified: Riggs 1890, p. 393].
The phrase's spread to Ojibwe use in the 1960s was borrowing, and contested (§0).

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

The cosmology and the gap are in the first Lakota file and are not repeated. One grammatical shadow is
new: English "all my relations" keeps the Lakota *my* faithfully; English "we are all related" does
not, because it puts a "we" where the Lakota speaker says "my". When the anchor chapter places
*mitakuyame:* above *yumi:* on a ladder of widening "we", it is the English gloss, not the Lakota
grammar, that supplies the "we".

## 6. Convergence-mapping

**For the anchor chapter's clusivity witnesses.** Lakota has a dedicated "you and I", and it is not
*mitákuye*. It is the verbal prefix **uŋ(k)-** [ũ(k)]:

- Riggs: "The dual is only of the first person: it includes the person speaking and the person spoken
  to, and has the form of the first person plural, but without the termination 'pi'"; subjective *uŋ*
  "(I and thou) we two", plural *uŋ…pi* "we" [verified: Riggs 1893, §13.2, p. 11; §18, p. 12; and §41.1,
  "mauŋni, we two walk", with *uŋ* inside *mani* "walk"; the OCR prints ŋ as "y" here].
- Wikipedia's table, citing Rood & Taylor (1996), gives first person *wa-* singular, *uŋ(k)-* dual,
  *uŋ(k)- … -pi* plural [verified: Wikipedia "Lakota language", Verb inflection].
- David Rood (2005): "even the 'dual inclusive' is applicable ONLY to agent/subject roles; you have to
  have the 'pi' with the object forms", for at least some speakers, and he believes the Handbook sketch
  makes the point [verified: SIOUAN list, 15 Dec 2005,
  https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/siouan/2005-December/006030.html; the sentence is Rood's
  own, from an earlier message quoted in that post]. Jan F. Ullrich, then of the Lakota Language
  Consortium (see §0), adds that the bare dual *un(k)* is used "only if 'I and you' are a subject of the
  action" [verified: https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/siouan/2005-December/006022.html, 14
  Dec 2005]. In the same thread Rood writes: "The point is that unyanpi is neither exclusive nor
  inclusive -- it is 'I and others'", and of the dual *unye* 'you and I went': "It's most common as an
  imperative -- unyin kte heci 'let's go', said to one person" [verified: same post, quoting Rood's
  earlier message; the list writes the nasal vowels with ⟨n⟩, i.e. *uŋyaŋpi*, *uŋye*, *uŋyiŋ*].
- WALS codes Lakhota "No inclusive/exclusive" both for independent pronouns (39A) and for verbal
  inflection (40A) [verified: https://wals.info/feature/39A.tab and 40A.tab]. My reading of that
  coding, which WALS does not explain for Lakhota: the plural *uŋ- … -pi* covers both inclusive and
  exclusive "we", so the opposition lives only in the dual, and the coding does not count it. Cysouw
  calls a dual found only in the inclusive a "minimal inclusive" [verified: WALS ch. 39, §1.5].

So Lakota is a witness for a dedicated "you and I" form, opaque (nothing in *uŋ-* recalls *wa-/ma-*
"I" or *ya-/ni-* "you"), bound, and restricted to subjects. It is not a witness for the Law of Visible
Constituents, and *mitákuye* is not a clusivity form at all.

## 8. Leads (excavation only — nothing here is coined, scored or forged)

1. **Lead: keep the attested noun whole.** A respelling that keeps *mitakuye* intact, rather than the
   unattested *mitakuya*, would remove departure 2. *Weaknesses:* it changes a Core word and a
   registered glyph; it still drops *oyás'iŋ*; and by §0 no respelling should be made before Lakota
   speakers have been asked whether YOUSPEAK should use the phrase at all.
2. **Lead: uŋ(k)- as the Siouan witness for "you and I".** If the chapter wants a Lakota witness for a
   dyadic inclusive, the honest one is *uŋ(k)-*. *Weaknesses:* it is opaque, so it supports clusivity but
   not visible constituents; it is a bound verbal prefix, not a word; it is restricted to subjects; and
   it is living grammar that would need the same custodial care.
3. **Lead: the ladder re-read.** Because the phrase says *my*, the top rung can be read as the first rung
   widened — I, standing in my own place, naming all as kin — rather than as a larger "we". *Weaknesses:*
   this is interpretation, not a finding about Lakota; whether the ladder should mean that is the
   language owner's decision.

_Mitákuye oyás'iŋ: one speaker, in the first person, naming everything as kin. The word "we" is the
listener's to add, and the Lakota did not add it._

## Verification (2026-11-10)

A second reader (a Claude subagent, not the excavator) re-opened every source tagged [verified]
above, independently of the excavator's copies.

**Re-opened and confirmed.**
- Riggs 1890, Internet Archive OCR. p. 455 lies under the running head "ta-ho'-sa — ta-le'-za" and
  has *ta-ku'-ye* "a relative — mitakuye, uŋkitakuyepi", *ta-ku'* "a relative, kindred", *ta'-ku*
  "something", *ta-ku'-ya* and *ta-ku'-ki-ći-ya-pi*. p. 393 has *o-wa'-siŋ* … "T., oyasiŋ". p. 397 has
  *o-ya'-siŋ* "T. all: i.q. owasiŋ". The preface has the Cleveland sentence.
- Riggs 1893: §13.2 and §15 on p. 11, §18 on p. 12, §§21–22 on pp. 14–15, §41.1.
- Wikipedia "Mitakuye Oyasin" and "Lakota language" (wikitext): Resolution No. 2012–343, adopted 13
  December 2012; the orthography disputes and the named custodians; the vowel, ejective and
  glottal-stop conventions; the prefix table beside the Rood & Taylor citation.
- NBC News, 3 June 2022.
- Both SIOUAN posts, and the WALS codings for Lakhota.
- Wiktionary *takúye*.

**Corrected.**
1. The stress quotation had been cut before "but often the first syllable can be stressed, and
   occasionally other syllables as well". It is restored, with the note that the rule is stated for
   monomorphemic words, which *mitákuye* is not.
2. Syllabification: *o.yás'.iŋ* is now *o.yá.s'iŋ*. The file's own ejective reading makes ⟨s'⟩ one
   onset consonant.
3. Riggs §41.1: *maŋni* is now *mauŋni*, as printed.
4. An unsourced acute had been added to *uŋkitakuyepi*. It is removed.
5. The table headword *owás'iŋ* was built on an unopened search result. It is now Riggs's *owásiŋ*.
6. The spellings table had credited *mitakuye … oyasiŋ* to Cleveland as a Teton form. Only *oyasiŋ*
   is marked "T.", and Riggs never prints the two together.
7. "Author of the *New Lakota Dictionary*" had been verified against NBC, which does not say it. It is
   moved to a [from memory] tag.
8. The second SIOUAN witness is named: Jan F. Ullrich.
9. Rood's "neither exclusive nor inclusive" remark on the plural is added from the same thread.
10. The reason for WALS's Lakhota coding is marked as inference.
11. The "dialect continuum" claim had been verified against WALS, which gives only the genus. It is
    re-tagged.
12. Tinker's "Amen" quotation is restored to full length.
