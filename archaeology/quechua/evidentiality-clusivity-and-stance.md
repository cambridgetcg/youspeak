# Evidentiality, clusivity and stance in Quechua — how a Quechua clause says how it knows, whom its "we" holds, and how hard it leans

*The first Quechua file (evidentials.md, 2026-04-24) carried no citations and taught that Quechua's third evidential is -chu. That spelling went on to become a YOUSPEAK morpheme at U+E14F, a canonized glyph, and a keyword of ORDO. This file goes back to the grammars. It finds the evidential set, variety by variety, and finds that -chu is something else: the enclitic of yes/no questions and of negation. It also opens what sits next to the evidentials and is often confused with them: the non-experienced past, the inclusive and exclusive "we", and the enclitics of certainty and emphasis. Its first duty is the correction. Its second is to say plainly where the sources complicate that correction, because they do.*

Some of the scholars this file leans on are native speakers writing about their own varieties. 2. **Reception, not extraction.** The evidential enclitics are working grammar in daily speech. YOUSPEAK borrows a shape and a distinction. It does not acquire the system, and it must not describe that system falsely, which the earlier file did.
3. **Consultation-desirability.** Before any liturgical or pedagogical use of a Quechua form, review by Quechua-speaking scholars is desirable. This applies with special force to the evidential set, because YOUSPEAK uses it to grade truth-claims, ORDO's "honesty algebra" among them.
4. **Retirement-condition.** If custodians ask, any YOUSPEAK use of a Quechua form is withdrawn at once.

**A hazard internal to this field.** Bernárdez (2020) studied evangelical translations into Quechua. The Ayacucho Bible he examined presents "todo, absolutamente todo" (everything, absolutely everything) as explicitly true, authoritative and reliable: in the passages he counted there are "cero casos de reportativos, sea -s(i) o el verbal -sqa-" and a very high rate of direct -m(i). YOUSPEAK's worship evidential -auth (U+E150) and its liturgical register stand at the same temptation: an evidential system bent so that a sacred text cannot be weighed. Name it, and do not repeat it.

## 1. The system in compact form

Most Quechua varieties have a small set of **evidential enclitics**. They attach to a word (often the focused constituent, or the verb by default), at most one per clause, and they are optional. A clause with none of them is not ungrammatical. In Cuzco, a clause with no evidential implicates direct evidence [verified: Faller 2006: 3, https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~rthomaso/lpw06/fallerpaper.pdf]. Bendezú-Araujo (2023), writing on Conchucos, likewise stresses that "these markers are not obligatory" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 76, 80, https://journals.iai.spk-berlin.de/index.php/indiana/article/download/3031/2518], and Bernárdez says the same of Quechua in general, "los marcadores no son obligatorios en quechua" [verified: Bernárdez 2020: 32]. One complication: Dedenbach-Salazar holds that a text must carry these suffixes at least once, so that their later absence is still evidentially marked at the level of discourse (2005: 11 and n.10, §2.2) [verified]. Optional per clause is not the same as meaningless when absent. In the Southern varieties the set is **direct -mi** (-n after a vowel in Cuzco), **reportative -si** (-s), and **conjectural -chá** (Cuzco) or **-cha ~ -ch** (Ayacucho). In Central varieties it is -mi, **-shi**, and a conjectural whose shape varies: **-chi** (Huallaga, South Conchucos), **-chr(a)** (Wanka), **-tri** (Yauyos). §2 gives the forms and loci.

**-chu is not in that set.** It is another enclitic in another position. It forms yes/no questions, appears with *mana* "not" in negation, and in some varieties forms disjunctions. It can stand in the same clause as the conjectural, and even in the same word: Ayacucho *ñuqa-chu-ch* "I guess maybe I will". It cannot be the conjectural's allomorph. §3.3 gives the evidence. Two modern analyses do put -chu in one class with the evidentials, though neither makes it a conjectural or inferential: Faller counts it in the same "focus set" (2006: 2 n.2), and Dedenbach-Salazar lists it inside her "Grupo Evidencia (también: validación)" as the yes/no-question and negative member (2005: 11–12). The sources also show why someone could misread it. -chu takes part in inferential constructions: Cuzco *-chus hina*, Ayacucho *-chusmi* "I think that", and Ayacucho rhetorical -chu with statement intonation, rendered "probably". A colonial grammarian groups chu with the dubitative particles as forms with which the verb "no afirma" (does not affirm). That is a partial basis for the old error, not a defence of it.

Structural marks the forge must preserve:

1. **A closed, mutually exclusive set of three, optional, in a slot of its own.** Solá & Parker's slot diagram for Ayacucho puts /-mI/, /-sI/, /-čA/ (and topic /-qa/) in one column, and /-ču/ in the column before it [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 108, page image read, https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41153663_0454]. Shimelman (Yauyos) states "only one evidential may occur per clause" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 272, https://archive.org/download/359-yauyos-quechua/(359)%20Yauyos%20Quechua.pdf].
2. **What the enclitics encode is disputed, and the dispute is the content.** There are three positions: source of information; the speaker's best possible grounds; and the speaker's commitment or assertional force (§3.2). González Holguín's grammar (1607), the oldest opened here, already calls -mi "señal de verbo afirmativo" (sign of an affirming verb), which is a commitment reading, not a source reading (§3.1).
3. **Evidentiality also lives in the past tense.** The *-sqa* (Southern) or *-ñaq / -naq* (Central) past presents an event as outside the speaker's experience, or as newly discovered. It is the mirative "it turns out" (§3.4).
4. **The first-person plural splits by whether the hearer is in it.** The exclusive is visibly *I + plural* (*ñuqa-y-ku*). The inclusive *ñuqa-nchik* is not visibly *I + you*. Its *-chik* piece recurs with the second person plural, and its history is debated (§3.5).

**Example (Cuzco), the paradigm sentence** [verified: Faller 2006: 3, ex. 1]:

```
Para-sha-n-mi/-si/-chá.
rain-prog-3-bpg/rep/conj
'It is raining.'
-mi ev: s sees that it is raining.
-si ev: s was told that it is raining.
-chá ev: s conjectures that it is raining.
```
IPA: /paɾaʃanmi/, /paɾaʃansi/, /paɾaʃanˈtʃa/. Bendezú-Araujo reproduces the same paradigm as three separate sentences, headed "Cuzco Quechua evidential system (Faller 2002, 122)" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 79; secondhand for Faller 2002, which was not opened, see ledger].

**The conjectural and -chu in one clause** [verified: Faller 2006: 11, ex. 18]:

```
Mana-n/-s/-chá para-sha-n-chu.
not-bpg/rep/conj rain-prog-3-pol
p=It isn't raining.
EV: s has direct/reportative/conjectural evidence that it isn't raining
```
IPA: /manan ~ manas ~ manaˈtʃa paɾaʃantʃu/. In this one clause, -chá sits on *mana* and -chu closes the verb. A morpheme cannot be its own co-occurring partner. This is the single most direct refutation of "-chu is the conjectural".

### 2.2 Ayacucho-Chanka (Quechua II, Southern)

Solá & Parker (1964) list thirteen Ayacucho enclitics [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 108, §8.1, page image read]. Among them: "/-ču/ (yes-no question, or negation, or rhetorical question), /-m/ ~ /-mi/ (comment of clause: speaker vouches for validity of statement), /-s/ ~ /-si/ (comment: speaker reports what he has heard), /-č/ ~ /-ča/ (comment: speaker is conjecturing)". The list also has /-puni/ "definitely", /-iki/ (apologetic), /-ya/ (regretful), and /-á/ "accent shift to final syllable of word; general emphasis".

| Form | IPA | Allomorphy | Meaning in the source | Attestation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| -mi | /mi/ ~ /m/ | /-m/ "following a vowel or preceding /-iki/ or /-Á/", /-mi/ elsewhere | "speaking from personal experience or conviction but is not translatable" | Solá & Parker 1964: 115, §8.207 [verified, page image] |
| -si | /si/ ~ /s/ | "distributed exactly as the allomorphs of /-mI/" | "heresay rather than personal experience or conviction"; "the only comment enclitic occurring in a clause with a verb in the quotative tense". Their own quotative-past example /aĨintam tokasqanki/ "you play well!" carries -m, so the rule is not exceptionless in their data (p. 59) | ibid. 116, §8.208; 59, §5.243 [verified] |
| -cha | /tʃa/ ~ /tʃ/ | "/-č/ and /-ča/ distributed exactly as the allomorphs of /-mI/ and /-sI/" | "indicating conjecture" | ibid. 117, §8.209 [verified] |

Dedenbach-Salazar's Ayacucho classification has the same core. -mi is "afirmativo, asertativo, experiencia personal", -si "reportativo", -cha "dubitativo, conjetural". **But she does not set -chu apart from them.** In her Cuadro 3, -chu "marcando la pregunta decisoria (de sí-no)", *manachu* "pregunta negativa" and "mana- …-chu negativo" are listed in the same "Grupo Evidencia (también: validación)" as -mi, -si and -cha. She describes that group as a continuum with the affirmative (eyewitness) at one end, questions in the centre and negation at the other [verified: Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 11–12, Cuadro 3]. Her Cuadro 5 labels every -chu combination "evidencia + evidencia" (-chum, -chus, -chuch, -chusmi) [verified: ibid. 17–18]. This is a broad sense of "evidence" (validation, the speaker's stance toward the statement), not a claim that -chu is conjectural or inferential: she glosses it only as the yes/no question and the negative. She also reports that Calvo Pérez (1993: 421–422) labels every Cuzco enclitic, -chu included, "evidenciales" in his suffix table, a model she calls inconsistent [verified secondhand: Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 8 n.9; Calvo Pérez not opened]. **Not an independent witness to Solá & Parker.** She takes her suffix order from "Dedenbach-Salazar Sáenz et al. 2002: 103, basado en Parker 1969: 78" and her functions from Parker (1969) and Soto Ruiz (1976) [verified: ibid. 12 n.12, 16, 17 n.17]. Gary J. Parker is Solá & Parker's co-author, and she cites the 1969 book as "Parker (1969 [ed. orig. 1964])" [verified: ibid. 5, survey list]. Her agreement with Solá & Parker is therefore largely descent, not corroboration. She also holds that marking a text with these suffixes "es obligatorio … por lo menos una vez" (is obligatory, at least once) at the level of discourse [verified: ibid. 11, Cuadro 3], which complicates the plain statement that the evidentials are optional (§1). **Allomorphy note.** For Ayacucho she analyses accented *-chá* as -cha + emphatic -á [verified: ibid. 19]. Faller treats Cuzco *-chá* as the basic conjectural. So the acute means different things in the two analyses. Do not import one into the other unexamined.

**Example (Ayacucho).** Solá & Parker give a transcription and translation but no interlinear gloss. The gloss line below is mine, built from their §§3.211, 5.241, 7.2307, 8.206 and 8.209. Their own words are only the transcription and the translation in quotation marks.

```
/ñuqačuč/                                  (Solá & Parker 1964: 117, §8.209)
ñuqa=ču=č
I=Q=CONJ                                   [gloss mine]
'I guess maybe I will'
```
IPA: /ɲuqatʃutʃ/. In one word, the polar enclitic is followed by the conjectural, in the slot order of p. 108; on p. 113 they state that in questions only /-mI/, /-sI/ or /-čA/ may follow /-ču/. **The same word appears a second time**, on p. 114, as an example of *rhetorical* -ču ("statement intonation … best rendered as suggestions or opinions with 'perhaps' or 'probably'"), translated there "'Maybe I.'" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 113, 114, page images]. So in their analysis the "maybe" of this word comes from the rhetorical use of -ču as well as from -č. It shows the two morphemes stacking; it does not show that -ču contributes nothing epistemic.

```

tařdi-man=ča qawa-yku-q ri-sa-q
late-ALL=CONJ look-YKU-AG go-1.FUT-FUT     [gloss mine; /qawayku-/ 'to visit' §7.2307, p. 89; /-sA/ 'first person future actor' §3.211, p. 20, before the tense suffix /-QA/ in its allomorph /-q/]

### 2.3 Huallaga-Huánuco (Quechua I, Central)

Kroeger, summarising Weber (1989), writes: "=mi marks 'direct' knowledge (e.g. eye-witness or personal experience); =shi marks hearsay; and =chi marks conjecture and/or inference" [verified: Kroeger, *Analyzing Meaning*, §17.2, https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Linguistics/Analyzing_Meaning_-_An_Introduction_to_Semantics_and_Pragmatics_(Kroeger)/17%3A_Evidentiality/17.02%3A_Some_common_types_of_evidential_systems]. The form table: -mi /mi/, -shi /ʃi/, -chi /tʃi/. Wikipedia's consonant table, citing Weber's grammar, gives Huallaga a single affricate /t͡ʃ/ [verified: Huallaga page above].

**Example (Huallaga)** [verified: Kroeger §17.2, ex. 2, citing Weber 1989: 421; Weber not opened]:

```
a. Qam-pis maqa-ma-shka-nki =mi.
   you-also hit-1.obj-prf-2.subj =direct
   'You also hit me (I saw and/or felt it).'
b. Qam-pis maqa-ma-shka-nki =shi.
   you-also hit-1.obj-prf-2.subj =hearsay
   '(Someone told me that) you also hit me (I was drunk and can't remember).'
c. Qam-pis maqa-ma-shka-nki =chi.
   you-also hit-1.obj-prf-2.subj =conject
   '(I infer that) you also hit me.'
   (I was attacked by a group of people, and I believe you were one of them).
```
IPA: /qampis maqamaʃkankimi/, /…ʃi/, /…tʃi/.

**What complicates this.**
- Shimelman, citing Weber (1989: 76), counts **four** Huallaga evidentials: "(-mi, -shi, -chi, snd -chaq)", typo as printed [verified: Shimelman 2017: 270 n.7]. I could not open Weber to see what the fourth form is. It may be the cognate of South Conchucos *=chaa* "mutual knowledge" (§2.5), but that is a guess.
- The SAILS database codes Huallaga as **not** marking "inferred evidentiality" morpho-syntactically (TAME4-6: N, source "Weber 1996"), and not marking "assumed evidentiality" either (TAME4-7: N, "pc vdKerke; not in Weber 1989") [verified: https://sails.clld.org/valuesets/TAME4-6-qub and …/TAME4-7-qub, and the JSON of both]. No comment explains this. **The same coding also says Huallaga does not mark secondhand information** (TAME4-3: N, source "Weber 1996"), while marking firsthand and non-firsthand information by affix (TAME4-1 and TAME4-2: AFF, sources "Weber 1989:421" and "Weber 1989:420") [verified: https://sails.clld.org/valuesets/TAME4-1-qub through …/TAME4-7-qub, HTML and JSON]. A "no" for secondhand sits badly with =shi "hearsay" in Kroeger's citation of Weber 1989: 421. The SAILS codes for Huallaga therefore look either narrowly defined or inconsistent, and this file gives them no weight either way.
- Negation: Wikipedia's Huallaga page illustrates vowel length with /kawa-ra-:-t͡ʃu/ "live-PST-1-NEG" 'I did not live', on a page whose main reference is Weber 1989; the example itself carries no citation [verified only as Wikipedia's example: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huallaga_Quechua, raw wikitext read 2026-11-10; tertiary]. The Huallaga polar-question form I did not establish from an opened source; I recall it is also -chu [from memory: not re-checked]. Contrast Ancash (§2.5), where they differ.

### 2.4 Wanka (Quechua I, Central)

The fullest source is Floyd (1999), which I could not open. A review of it, seen only as a search-result snippet, describes a chapter on "the conjecture evidential -chr(a)" at pp. 93–122 [search-result snippet of Curnow's review on Project MUSE; the page itself was behind a bot check, so this is unverified]. Wikipedia credits Floyd (1999: 95) with -chr(a) relaying "the speaker's non-commitment to the truth-value of the statement" [verified only as Wikipedia's wording: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechuan_languages, raw wikitext read 2026-11-10; Floyd not opened].

**Example (Wanka), as reproduced by Wikipedia from Floyd 1999: 103** [tertiary; verified only as Wikipedia reproduces it]:

```
kuti-mu-n'a-qa-chr ni-ya-ami
return-AFAR-3FUT-now-CONJ say-IMPV-1-DIR
I think they will probably come back.
```
IPA: only partly given, /kutimu…ʈʂ niyaːmi/. I withhold IPA for ‹n'a›. The apostrophe probably writes the glottal reflex of *q that I recall for Huanca [from memory: not re-checked], but Wikipedia's consonant table lists no glottal stop. The first word's five segments and five labels line up one to one; the mismatch is in the second word, where *ni-ya-ami* has three segments for four labels (say-IMPV-1-DIR), so ‹ami› presumably holds the first person plus -mi (QI marks the first person by vowel length, Shimelman 2017: 8 [verified]); that segmentation is my inference. Treat this example as unreliable until it is checked against Floyd.

**Wanka polar question.** Wikipedia, citing Floyd (1999: 89), says: "The question marker in Wanka Quechua, -chun, is derived from the negative –chu marker and the direct evidential." ### 2.5 Comparanda: South Conchucos (Ancash) and Yauyos

**South Conchucos (Quechua I).** Hintz's abbreviation list gives DIR "-m(i)/-mer direct evidential", DIR.YET "-ran direct episodic evidential", RPT "-sh(i) reportative evidential", CNJ "-chi conjectural evidential", MUT "-cha: mutual evidential", APP "-chir/-char appeal evidential", DUB "-tsura: dubitive evidential", SURE "-ri surely evidential" [verified: Hintz 2011: xxi–xxiii, https://escholarship.org/content/qt6wb842zj/qt6wb842zj_noSplash_d11bb20edbb4fc881134e65f42f30bbb.pdf, read from the copy downloaded in this workflow; a fresh fetch on re-check was refused by the server]. The same list has NEG "-tsu negative", NEG.APP "-ra:ku dubitive negative with appeal", Q.P "-ku polar question (yes/no)", and **LOC "-chu/-cho: locative case"** [verified: same]. In Ancash, then, negation takes -tsu (that -tsu is the cognate of Southern -chu is [from memory: not re-checked]); yes/no questions take -ku; and a word ending in -chu means "in, at". Bendezú-Araujo's Huari (Conchucos) data show the same -ku: *aw-ku mana-ku?* "yes-q no-q" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 85, ex. 4]. IPA: /mi/, /meɾ/, /ɾan/, /ʃi/, /tʃi/, /tʃaː/, /tʃiɾ/, /tsuɾaː/, /ɾi/, /tsu/, /ɾaːku/, /ku/, /tʃu/, /aw ku mana ku/ (‹e› in -mer kept as written; whether it is a phoneme here I did not check). Shimelman reports an anonymous reviewer: "in Huaylas Q, negative -tsu is distinguished from polar question -ku" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 253 n.4].

[verified]. IPA: /kanan oːɾa ʎakijkan papaːnin imanatʃi/.

Hintz & Hintz (2017) propose a five-term South Conchucos system: *=mi* "assertion of individual knowledge", *=chaa* "confirmation/assertion of mutual knowledge", *=chi* "individual conjecture", *=cher* "mutual conjecture, appeal for consensus", *=shi* "reported information" [verified secondhand: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 80, ex. 2, adapting Hintz & Hintz 2017: 91; Hintz & Hintz not opened]. **Contested.** Working on Conchucos Quechua from Huari province, which he treats as the variety Hintz & Hintz describe ("a five-term system for CQ"), Bendezú-Araujo reports that his team "could not elicit the 'individual-mutual' distinction". Speakers took *=mi/=chaa* "to be synonymous" and "didn't recognise =cher as a unit of their variety" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 85].

**Yauyos (mixed QI/QII).** The three evidentials are "direct -mi …, reportative -shi …, and conjectural -tri"; "Following a short vowel, these are realized as -m, sh, and -tr" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 270]. Each can take the modifiers -ik and -iki, giving nine members [verified: ibid. 18, 272]. -chu "indicates absolute … and disjunctive questions …, negation …, and disjunction" [verified: ibid. 253]. That -tr is retroflex /ʈʂ/ is [from memory: not re-checked].

'That's why she wouldn't have waited for me'

For Huallaga -kuna see Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 270, citing Weber 1983 [verified]. For the Yauyos 1pl forms see Shimelman 2017: 37–38; for Yauyos "-shqa" rather than -naq, ibid. 8 [verified].

**Sibling distinctions.** -chá is not -chu: one is a comment enclitic that sets the clause's evidential grade, the other a polarity enclitic that makes it a question or a negation. -chus hina is not -chá. Faller treats -chá as conjecture, "My hunch is", with "some reason to believe p but no solid external evidence". She treats -chus hina as "I guess, I think, apparently", the form needed when the speaker has partial evidence (§3.2). -puni is not -mi. -puni sits closer to the stem and, in elicitation, can fall under negation and embedding, where -mi cannot (Faller 2006: 5, 10–12; she marks the -puni data as tentative, §3.6). -sqa is not -si. One is a tense, the other an enclitic, and they co-occur: Ayacucho *huk-si ka-sqa* "once upon a time there was…" (Solá & Parker 1964: 116).

## 3. Primary attestations and scholarly references

### 3.1 The colonial witness: González Holguín (1607)

Diego González Holguín's *Gramática y arte nueva* (Lima, 1607) describes Cuzco "lengua general". I read it in two OCR texts on archive.org: the 1607 Lima edition (gramaticayartenu00gonz) and the 1842 Genoa reprint (gramticayartenue00gon, pages cited below). OCR noise and spacing are normalised in the quotations; wording is as OCR'd, and the 1607 text is used where the reprint departs from it.

- **-mi affirms; -chu asks; ch/cha doubt.** "la m, o mi es señal de verbo afirmativo, y como la partícula chu es señal de interrogativos y siempre se pone en habiendo interrogación" (the m or mi is the sign of an affirming verb, and as the particle chu is the sign of interrogatives and is always put where there is a question). Then: the m/mi is used in every indicative tense "sino es cuando el verbo de indicativo es interrogativo, o dubitativo" (except when the indicative verb is interrogative or dubitative), "con ychach, o ch, o cha, o chu, que entonces no affirma" (with ychach, or ch, or cha, or chu, for then it does not affirm). The 1607 OCR reads "o cha, o chu". The 1842 reprint OCR reads "o cha, o cha" [verified: 1842 reprint p. 71 (Lib. II cap. 3); 1607 OCR, Lib. II, folio not determined; https://archive.org/details/gramticayartenue00gon and https://archive.org/details/gramaticayartenu00gonz]. **This complicates the correction a little.** Holguín puts chu in one list with the dubitative ch/cha, as the particles that withdraw affirmation. He does not equate them: he has just named chu "señal de interrogativos".
- **ch/cha "quizá", and chu "si" (whether).** In Lib. I cap. 15, "Algunas partículas son dobladas, una para con una vocal, y otra compañera para con consonante o dos vocales … como (ch) o (cha)" (some particles come in pairs, one for use after a vowel and a companion for after a consonant or two vowels, … like (ch) or (cha)); "doubled" here means paired allomorphs. His examples: "Nocach, quizá yo" (perhaps I), "Pich, quien será, o no se quien" (who might it be, or I don't know who). Then, "Con Chun o Chuch": "Pichum, si alguno" (whether anyone); "Ymachuch, si alguna cosa por ventura o quizá" (whether perhaps anything); "Camchum, si tu" (whether you); "Camchuch, si quizá tu" (whether perhaps you) [verified: 1842 reprint pp. 57–58; 1607 OCR "ñocach: Quiça yo", "CON (CHVN) O (CHVCH.)"]. IPA: /ɲuqatʃ/, /pitʃ/, /pitʃum/, /imatʃutʃ/, /qamtʃum/, /qamtʃutʃ/. So -chu plus -mi gives "whether (I'm asking)", and -chu plus -cha gives "whether perhaps". The two stack. They are not one morpheme.
- **The mirative *kasqa*.** "lo que se dice con casca … no se sabia antes y de nuevo se sabe, o de repente se conoce" (what is said with casca was not known before and is newly known, or suddenly recognised). Holguín's example: "como cuando viene alguno de lejos y todos piensan que era Juan y era Pedro cuando llega y se desengañan, dicen Pedrom casca" (as when someone comes from far off and everyone thinks it was Juan, and it was Pedro when he arrives, and they are undeceived; they say Pedrom casca). He adds: "sirve casca para contar nuevas, y para referir un ejemplo, o enseñar cosas del cielo, o de virtudes, o cosas ocultas, o sucesos repentinos, o cosas inauditas, que no suele saber el auditorio" (casca serves to tell news, to relate an example, or to teach things of heaven, virtues, hidden things, sudden events or unheard-of things that the audience does not usually know) [verified: 1842 reprint p. 70; same in the 1607 OCR]. IPA: /kasqa/, /pedɾom kasqa/.
- **Inclusive and exclusive.** "canchic decimos incluyendo cuando aquellos con quien hablamos hacen con nosotros lo que dice el verbo, y caycu decimos exclusive cuando … no hacen con nosotros lo que dice el verbo" (we say canchic inclusively when those we speak to do with us what the verb says, and caycu exclusively when they do not). His examples: "christianomcanchic … decimos á otros que también son cristianos con nosotros, mas á los que no lo son decimos christianomcaycu" (christianomcanchic we say to others who are Christians with us, but to those who are not we say christianomcaycu). Spaniards "cuando hablan á los indios dicen viracochamcayco" (when they speak to the Indians say viracochamcayco). Men say "ccarimcaycu" to boys and women [verified: 1842 reprint pp. 70–71; the 1607 OCR has "chriſtianomcaycu"]. In Lib. I the 1607 text adds that priests speaking to laymen say "ñocayco padre cuna" (we the fathers) [verified: 1607 OCR, Lib. I, plural of ñoca]. IPA: /kantʃik/, /kajku/, /qarim kajku/, /ɲuqajku/. The colonial exclusive is a boundary-marker of religion, race, gender and office.

### 3.2 What the evidentials encode: source, grounds, commitment

**(a) Source of information.** This is the textbook view: direct, reported, conjectured. Kroeger's Huallaga triplet follows Weber (§2.3). Solá & Parker's -si "reports what he has heard" is also a source label. Bendezú-Araujo lists Weber (1989, ch. 21), Muysken (1995), Faller (2002), Cerrón-Palomino (2003, ch. 9) and others as describing =mi/=shi/=chi as evidentials. He names Floyd (1996; 1997), Adelaar (1977), Behrens (2012) and Grzech as the exceptions [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 76 n.2].

**(b) Best possible grounds (Faller).** Faller (2002) "analyzes -mi (allomorph -n) as a marker of 'best possible grounds', which amounts to having direct evidence in cases in which an event can be directly observed" [verified: Faller 2006: 3]. Bendezú-Araujo quotes the definition from the dissertation: =mi indicates that "the speaker acquired the information from the best source out of all the sources a normal person is expected to have access to, for the kind of information conveyed" [verified secondhand: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 79–80, quoting Faller 2002: 135]. Bernárdez quotes the extension to authority: "a speaker who uses -mi claims to have authority over the information conveyed" [verified secondhand: Bernárdez 2020: 31, quoting Faller 2002: 135]. In Faller's analysis the evidentials are illocutionary modifiers that add sincerity conditions; they are not propositional operators. They take wide scope over negation and do not embed in conditional antecedents. Conditional *-man* readily embeds and falls under negation. *-puni* does so only in elicitation, and Faller says the evidence there is less clear [verified: Faller 2006: 9–12, exx. 14, 16, 18–21]. She grades her own evidence: very strong for the reportative as illocutionary, "somewhat less strong" for the direct and conjectural, and "perhaps least convincing" for -puni as propositional [verified: Faller 2006: 16]. -chá "may therefore be classed as both an evidential and an epistemic modal" [verified: Faller 2006: 4]. Bendezú-Araujo puts this as asserting "◊p", with the locus Faller 2002: 171–189 [verified secondhand: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 80 n.7]. Faller later gave a possible-worlds semantics of the three, in work whose title and venue I verified but did not read (Faller 2020, *Evidentials and Modals*, Brill, pp. 41–68, doi 10.1163/9789004436701_004) [verified: Crossref metadata only].

**(c) Commitment and assertional force.**
- **Weber.** Dedenbach-Salazar glosses Ayacucho -cha "dubitativo, conjetural", with a note: "Tal vez también evasión de comprometerse (Weber 1986: 153 para el quechua I)" (perhaps also evasion of commitment) [verified secondhand: Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 12 n.13; Weber 1986 not opened]. Wikipedia, citing Floyd (1999: 57), describes Wanka -mi as "strong personal conviction" [verified only as Wikipedia's wording]. Beyond this, my picture of Weber 1986 is from memory: an "information perspective" account in which the source is the speaker's own experience and choosing -mi makes the speaker answerable for the claim [from memory: not re-checked]. It needs the chapter itself, *Evidentiality* (Chafe & Nichols eds., 1986), which I did not open.
- **The earliest descriptions opened here are commitment readings.** (Domingo de Santo Tomás's grammar of 1560, older than Holguín's and cited by Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 269 [verified], was not opened.) Holguín's -mi is "señal de verbo afirmativo" (§3.1). Solá & Parker's -mi is "speaker vouches for validity of statement" [verified: 1964: 108]. Dedenbach-Salazar reports that Calvo Pérez (1993: 103) describes -mi as "indicador enfático del compromiso de verdad por parte del hablante" (emphatic marker of the speaker's commitment to truth) [verified secondhand: Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 8 n.9].
- **Faller on the reportative.** "a speaker uttering a declarative sentence with the reportative evidential does not need to be committed to the reported proposition φ … Such utterances are therefore not assertions of φ". Her example: "Pay-kuna=s qulqi-ta saqiy-wa-n. Mana=má, ni un sol-ta saqi-sha-wa-n=chu." "'They left me money (I was told)'. (But) no, they didn't leave me one sol." [verified: Faller 2019: 8:2, ex. 1–2, citing Faller 2002: 191]. And: "RDSs in CQ do not conventionally encode any degree of commitment by the speaker (Faller 2002, 2007)" [verified: ibid. 8:7]. The same paper notes that "All three evidentials can occur in wh-questions, but only the reportative in yes/no questions (Cusihuaman 2001)" [verified: ibid. 8:5 n.5].
- **Against BPG.** Behrens (2012) reanalyses =mi as marking a "strong" assertion, one that "signals that the speaker intends to convince their interlocutors" [verified secondhand: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 81]. For Upper Napo Kichwa, Grzech (2016b; 2020) shows =mi embedded under "seem" ("It seems my father is coming") and argues for "epistemic primacy", a "relative right to know" [verified secondhand: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 82]. Bendezú-Araujo notes this would be impossible in Cuzco, "where =mi is not allowed in partial evidence contexts, and the marker =chu(s) hina would be needed instead (Faller 2002, 174)" [verified secondhand: same page]. His own Conchucos analysis treats =mi as "an assertional reinforcer and that of =chi as an assertional mitigator or hedge", which "signals that the speaker is not ready to fully commit to the truth of the proposition" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 76].

**Focus as well.** Faller, following Muysken (1995), notes that the enclitics "also mark focus" [verified: Faller 2006: 2 n.2]. Bendezú-Araujo lists Wölck (1972), Adelaar (1977), Weber (1989), Nuckolls (1993), Muysken (1995) and others as calling them "(contrastive) focus markers" [verified: Bendezú-Araujo 2023: 79 n.6].

**What the forge should take from this.** The Quechua set is not a pure source-tagger. For about four centuries its grammarians have described it in terms of the speaker's warrant and willingness to vouch, as well as in terms of source. A YOUSPEAK reading of -mi/-si as "witnessed / reported" is a defensible simplification. A reading of the third term as "inferred from evidence" fits *-chus hina* and the Central conjecturals (Kroeger's "conjecture and/or inference") better than it fits Cuzco -chá, which Faller treats as a hunch.

### 3.3 -chu in questions and negation

- **Polar questions.** Ayacucho: "/-ču/ is added to the word or phrase whose referent is in doubt, and question intonation … accompanies the clause". Examples: /kay ñančum ayakučuman riq/ "does this road go to Ayacucho?" with -m, "with speaker's feeling that it does"; /manaču hamunki?/ "Won't you come?" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 113–116]. Cuzco: "presidente-man-puni-chu hayku-nki? 'Are you certainly going to enter as president?'", which Faller constructs by modifying a sentence from Valderrama & Escalante (1982: 34) [verified: Faller 2006: 15 n.21, 16 ex. 28]. Yauyos: "iskwila-man trura-shu-rqa-nki-chu mama-yki / school-ALL put-2.OBJ-PST-2-Q mother-3 / 'Did your mother put you in school?'" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 253, ex. 1]. IPA: /kaj ɲantʃum ajakutʃuman ɾiq/, /manatʃu hamunki/, /iskwilaman ʈʂuɾaʃuɾqankitʃu mamajki/.
- **Negation.** "A negation always contains a negator /mana/ (general) or /ama/ (prohibitory)". The negator usually takes the comment enclitic, while the negated "substantival or verbal phrase … adds /-ču/ in cross reference": /manam wasiču./ "Its not a house." [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 114, page image]. Cuzco: "mana-n ri-y-man-chu. / not-bpg go-1-cond-pol / 'I can't go.'" [verified: Faller 2006: 6, ex. 7, citing Cusihuaman 2001: 169]. IPA: /manam wasitʃu/, /manan ɾijmantʃu/. In Quechua, then, "The dokimance did not succeed-chu" (grammars/evidentials/manifesto.md:110) would read as a plain negation, not as "I infer that it did not".
- **Disjunction.** Yauyos *Mario-chu ka-rqa Julian-chu ka-rqa* "It was either Mario or Julian" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 254, ex. 7].
- **Where -chu does reach toward inference.** These are the complications, stated in full:
  1. Ayacucho rhetorical questions "carry regular statement intonation" and "are usually best rendered as suggestions or opinions with 'perhaps' or 'probably'": /tabařdiĨuču hapirusunki./ "Probably sunstroke got you." [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 114, page image]. IPA: /tabaʐdiʎutʃu hapiɾusunki/. -chu with falling intonation gives a hedged statement. The hedge comes from the question-form put to work as an opinion, not from an evidential value of -chu.
  2. Ayacucho *-chusmi*, which Solá & Parker call an "idiomatic combination" and Dedenbach-Salazar analyses as -chu + -si + -mi (2005: 18, Cuadro 5), "indicates opinion with conviction, translatable usually as 'I think that'": /manačusmi./ "I don't think so." [verified: ibid. 115]. Dedenbach-Salazar glosses it "al hablante le parece ser probable que la acción se realice" (it seems probable to the speaker that the action takes place) [verified: 2005: 18].
  3. Cuzco *-chus hina* (-chu + -s + *hina* "like") "means roughly I guess, I think, apparently", is "mutually exclusive with the three main evidential enclitics", and was hypothesized to be a fourth evidential [verified: Faller 2006: 2–3 n.4]. Faller (2019) calls it "arguably an inferential (Faller 2011)", with the example "Noqa-manta=qa rima-y-lla=chu sino ka-n-man / I-abl=top speak-inf-lim=dub be-3-cond / 'For myself, this seems just talk.'" (Valderrama & Escalante 1982: 25) [verified: Faller 2019: 8:5 n.4 and 8:7, ex. 8]. Faller herself says its inclusion "requires further research" [verified: 2006: 3 n.4].
  4. Holguín lists chu with the dubitative ch/cha as particles with which the verb "no affirma" (§3.1).
  5. Solá & Parker give the same word /ñuqačuč/ twice: as rhetorical -ču, "'Maybe I.'" (p. 114), and under the conjectural, "'I guess maybe I will'" (p. 117) (§2.2).
  6. Faller counts -chu in the same "focus set" as the evidentials (2006: 2 n.2), and her 2019 paper calls the evidentials "a paradigm of evidential focus enclitics" (8:5) [verified]. The kinship is real at the level of focus marking.
  7. Dedenbach-Salazar's classification lists -chu, *manachu* and *mana … -chu* inside the "Grupo Evidencia (también: validación)", on a continuum running from affirmation through questions to negation, and labels -chu "evidencia" in every fixed combination (2005: 11–12, 17–18) [verified]. Calvo Pérez (1993) is reported as calling all Cuzco enclitics "evidenciales" [verified secondhand: Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 8 n.9]. So "evidential" in a broad, validational sense has been applied to -chu in print. Neither author calls it inferential or conjectural.

  None of these makes -chu, alone and in its own slot, a member of the evidential set in the narrow sense (direct / reportative / conjectural). They do show that "-chu (with interrogative register)", as the old file put it, points at something real: Quechua builds inference-hedges out of the question enclitic. The old file then misnamed that as the evidential itself.
- **Ancash differs.** There the polar question is -ku and the negative is -tsu. -chu is the locative (§2.5).

### 3.4 The non-experienced and mirative past: -sqa, -ñaq/-naq

Cerrón-Palomino calls it "el llamado pasado narrativo o mítico (en sus variantes -ñaq o -šqa)" (the so-called narrative or mythic past, in its variants -ñaq or -šqa) [verified: Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 265, page image, https://journals.iai.spk-berlin.de/index.php/indiana/article/download/1794/1432]. Shimelman lists "-naq in place of -shqa to form the narrative past" among the traits that set QI apart [verified: Shimelman 2017: 8]. So **-ñaq/-naq** is the Central form and **-sqa/-shqa** the Southern and Yauyos form. Hintz derives South Conchucos "Narrative past -na: (and -na:=ka)" from "a periphrastic construction in which the form *-ña-q was followed by the auxiliary *ka- inflected with a past suffix plus a person marker" [verified: Hintz 2011: 85]. IPA: /ɲaq/, /naq/, /sqa/, /ʃqa/, /naː/.

- **Ayacucho, "The Quotative Past".** "This tense is regularly used in story-telling, and in conversation it indicates that the speaker is not responsible for the event, that he was unaware of the fact of, or the real significance of, the action at the time it occurred or began." Examples: /aĨintam tokasqanki/ "you play well!"; /ñama mikučkasqankiña/ "Oh, you're already eating"; /čay sipasta mučasqani/ "I kissed that girl (I mistook her for another, or did it while I was drunk, or dreamt it, etc.)" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 58–59, §5.243; p. 58 page image]. IPA: /aʎintam tokasqanki/, /ɲama mikutʃkasqankiɲa/, /tʃaj sipasta mutʃasqani/. The first two are the mirative "it turns out". The third is the non-volitional or non-experienced use.
- **Cuzco.** In a news story, -sqa (glossed "nx.pst") co-occurs with the reportative: "Hinaspa chay-pi=s, qonqay, hap'i-ra-pu-sqa-ku kinsa-manta. … 'And then, there, all of a sudden, they grabbed him, between three.'" [verified: Faller 2019: 8:6, ex. 5a]. A likely surprise reading: "papa chakra-ta-qa chikchi-ru-sha-sqa-táq / potato field-OBJ-TOP hail-OUT-PROG-PST.P-EMPH / 'It had been hailing on the potato field!'" [verified: Hintz 2011: 194, ex. 263]. Hintz cites this example and the Huallaga one below to show -ru/-rqu co-occurring with a tense marker, not to discuss -sqa or -naq; "surprise" is my reading of his exclamation marks, and the original sources of both examples are not named on that page. Faller's dedicated study is "The Deictic Core of 'Non-Experienced Past' in Cuzco Quechua", *Journal of Semantics* 21(1): 45–85 (2004) [verified: Crossref metadata only, doi 10.1093/jos/21.1.45; not read]. Bernárdez quotes Hurtado de Mendoza (2002: 91), "siguiendo a Cerrón Palomino": -sqa is used "para indicar una acción ejecutada en el pasado sin control por parte del hablante … el hablante no asume responsabilidad" (to indicate an action carried out in the past without the speaker's control; the speaker takes no responsibility) [verified secondhand: Bernárdez 2020: 32]. IPA: /hapʼiɾapusqaku/, /tʃiktʃiɾuʃasqaˈtaq/ (the ejective in hap'i is as spelled).
- **Huallaga -naq.** "i chay-man-qa tiya-n-ta puñu-rqa-chi-naq chay sonso / and that-GOAL-TOP aunt-3-OBJ sleep-OUT-CAUS-PST.N that stooge / 'And the stooge made his aunt sleep there!'" [verified: Hintz 2011: 194, ex. 262]. IPA: /puɲuɾqatʃinaq/.
- **South Conchucos -na:.** "In terms of evidentiality, -na: presents a situation as outside the experience of conversational participants or participants within the narrative itself". On the main storyline it "directly reports surprise value" [verified: Hintz 2011: 145, §6.4]. Example: "unay-qa ukush-pis parla-na:-shi / long.ago-TOP mouse-EVEN speak-PST.N-RPT / [Long ago even mice could speak, they say.]" [verified: ibid. 143, ex. 190].

### 3.5 Clusivity: ñuqanchik against ñuqayku, -nchik against -yku

- **Forms.** Ayacucho: "/ñuqa/ 'I' is inflected for number, the plural distinguishing inclusive /ñuqančik/ 'we' and exclusive /ñuqayku/ 'we'" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 37, §4.151, OCR]. Their person suffixes: "/-NINčik/ refers to a group which includes the addressee; when it is not preceded by the second person suffix (or imperative /-y/, or conditional /-waq/) this group also includes the speaker: /wasinčik/ 'our (incl.) house' … /rikunčik/ 'we (incl.) see'; /wasikičik/ 'your (pl.) house' … /rikunkičik/ 'you-all see'". And: "/-ku/ refers to a group which excludes the addressee; it follows /-NIY/ for first person plural exclusive, and follows /-NIn/ or zero for third person plural: /wasiyku/ 'our (excl.) house' … /rikuniku/ 'we (excl.) see'; /wasinku/ 'their house' … /rikunku/ 'they see'. /-ku/ refers only to humans." [verified: ibid. 19–20, §3.211, page images]. Futures: /rikusaqku/ "we (excl.) will see"; /rikusun/ or /rikusunčik/ "we (incl.) will see; let's see" [verified: ibid. 58, page image]. Cuzco: "mana-n ri-sunchis-chu / not-bpg go-1inc.fut-pol" [verified: Faller 2006: 10, ex. 16]. IPA: /ɲuqantʃik/, /ɲuqajku/, /wasintʃik/, /ɾikuntʃik/, /wasikitʃik/, /ɾikunkitʃik/, /wasijku/, /ɾikuniku/, /wasinku/, /ɾikunku/, /ɾikusaqku/, /ɾikusuntʃik/, /ɾisuntʃistʃu/.
- **Is the inclusive visibly compositional?** The exclusive is: *ñuqa-y-ku* and *wasi-y-ku* are 1st person -y plus -ku, the same -ku that pluralises the third person (*wasi-n-ku*). Cerrón-Palomino adds that Cajamarca *ñuqa-y-kuna* shows "que la forma sureña -y-ku refleja una abreviación de aquélla (la misma que se nota en la pluralización de la tercera persona: -n-ku)" (that the southern -y-ku is an abbreviation of it, the same seen in the third-person plural -n-ku). He also notes that the south uses -y-ku and the other varieties *ñuqa-kuna* [verified: Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 269]. The inclusive is only partly compositional. The pronoun *ñuqa-nchik* keeps *ñuqa* "I" visible. Solá & Parker give the meaning "refers to a group which includes the addressee" to the whole suffix /-NINčik/, and the speaker is included only "when it is not preceded by the second person suffix"; the same -čik closes *-yki-čik* "your (pl.)". Whether *-nchik* contains a first-person marker is an analysis, not a given. Cerrón-Palomino says it does not, and says so against an older view: "Descartamos en tal sentido el entronque que suele hacerse desde antiguo entre dicha -n y el marcador de primera persona -ni" (we therefore reject the link, commonly made since long ago, between this -n and the first-person marker -ni) [verified: 1987: 273, page image]. On the older view the inclusive would be partly first-person-marked. Cerrón-Palomino: "comparando la segunda persona plural del QII -yki-čik/-nki-čik con la forma del inclusivo -n-čik, salta a la vista el carácter bimorfémico de esta última" (comparing the QII second person plural -yki-čik/-nki-čik with the inclusive -n-čik, the bimorphemic character of the latter is obvious). The -n "podría ser el mismo protomorfema que fue interpretado como 'tercera persona'" (could be the same proto-morpheme that was interpreted as 'third person') [verified: 1987: 273, page image]. He proposes, as a hypothesis ("no parece muy aventurado", it does not seem too adventurous), that -čik comes from "la partícula čika, que indica abundancia" (the particle čika, which indicates abundance). Later "la secuencia -n-čik fue reinterpretada como inclusión, preservando el segundo elemento su carácter plural sólo en la segunda persona" (the sequence -n-čik was reinterpreted as inclusion, the second element keeping its plural sense only in the second person) [verified: 1987: 271, 274, page images]. As colonial evidence he cites Holguín's *camchic* "vosotros malos hombres" (you wicked men), with "gravedad, o desprecio del otro" (gravity, or contempt for the other) [verified: 1987: 271, citing "González Holguín ([1607] 1848: 40-41)", his date for the reprint; the reprint read for §3.1 is dated 1842]. So the Quechua inclusive is **addressee-marked but not "you"-worded**: it has no *qam* "you" inside it. The Law of Visible Constituents in grammars/ankyrance/ is not met by Quechua.
- **Number or person, and possibly borrowed.** Cerrón-Palomino reports Mannheim's conclusion that "mientras en el aimara la diferencia se instala en el sistema de persona, en el quechua ella se codifica en el sistema de número" (in Aymara the difference sits in the person system, in Quechua it is coded in the number system). He does not simply accept it: "Sin embargo, si bien es cierto que tal apreciación podría corresponder al estado sincrónico actual … históricamente la situación parece haber sido diferente" (however, while that view may fit the present synchronic state, historically the situation seems to have been different). In his reconstruction, in *\*ñuqa-n-čik* the inclusion was carried by *\*-čik*, only partly read as plural. He then asks whether the inclusive/exclusive distinction "fue introducida como efecto de una convergencia" (was introduced through convergence) with Aru, the two families having been in contact "por espacio de por lo menos dos milenios" (for at least two millennia). He also notes that the distinction is common to at least three Andean families, "la aru, la quechua y la uru-chipaya" [verified: 1987: 274, page image]. **Consequence for convergence counts:** Quechua and Aymara clusivity cannot be counted as independent witnesses; he poses convergence as a question, not a finding. Whether Uru-Chipaya's split is also a contact feature was not examined here.
- **Not every variety keeps the split.** The colombo-ecuatoriana varieties and those of the Peruvian Oriente "han perdido la segunda forma" (have lost the second, exclusive, form) [verified: Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 269, OCR].
- **Central varieties.** Yauyos has "ñuqa-nchik (dual), ñuqa-nchik-kuna (inclusive), and ñuqa-kuna (exclusive)". But "In practice, except in ch, ñuqa-nchik is employed with dual, inclusive and exclusive interpretations to the virtual complete exclusion of the other two forms" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 37–38]. QI generally uses *ñuqakuna* for the exclusive [verified: ibid. 8]. South Conchucos marks "1st/2nd person inclusive" with -ntsi: ~ -ntsik [verified: Hintz 2011: xxi]. IPA: /ɲuqakuna/, /ntsiː/, /ntsik/.

### 3.6 Certainty and emphasis where they bear on stance: -puni, -má, -yá

- **-puni.** Cuzco: "Para-sha-n-puni. / rain-prog-3-def / 'It is certainly raining.'"; "Mayu killa-pi-qa qasa-mu-n-puni-n. … 'In May, it always gets freezing cold.'" (Cusihuaman 2001: 244) [verified: Faller 2006: 5–6, exx. 3a, 8]. Unlike the evidentials, -puni can be embedded and can fall under negation, though Faller hedges both: "Mana Juan-puni-chu ka-n-man ka-rqa-n. … 'It is not certain that it was Juan.'" [verified: ibid. 12, ex. 21b]. She adds that this "must be taken with a bit of caution": the one speaker who accepted it did so "only rather reluctantly", and out of context the sentence "would normally be interpreted as 'It certainly wasn't Juan'" (ibid. 12 n.15). In naturally occurring examples she found -puni only with wide scope over negation (ibid. 11, ex. 20), and her consultant was "much happier" embedding -man than epistemic -puni (ibid. 10) [verified]. Ayacucho: /-puni/ "definitely" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 108]. Dedenbach-Salazar: "convicción personal, certeza … la certeza es la del sujeto de la oración" (personal conviction, certainty; the certainty is the sentence subject's), and "En términos de evidencia es lo contrario de -cha" (in evidential terms it is the opposite of -cha) [verified: 2005: 15–16]. Yauyos: "paqarin-puni-m ri-shaq / tomorrow-CERT-EVD go-1.FUT / 'I'm going to go precisely tomorrow'", attested only in one dialect [verified: Shimelman 2017: 263]. IPA: /puni/, /paɾaʃanpuni/, /paqaɾinpunim ɾiʃaq/.
- **-má.** Cuzco: "Mana=má, ni un sol-ta saqi-sha-wa-n=chu. / no=impr not one Sol-acc leave-prog-1o-3=neg" [verified: Faller 2019: 8:37, ex. 37]. Faller does not analyse it as certainty: she glosses it "impr" (impressive) and calls it "the surprise enclitic =má", a piece of the "perspectival language" that typically accompanies a speaker's denial of a report (8:20, 8:44) [verified]. In Ayacucho it decomposes: /-mI/ is /-m/ "preceding … /-Á/" [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 115]. The example: "/qarikamamá kanqa; amamá warmiču/ 'they'll all be boys; not (I hope) girls!'" [verified: ibid. 116, page image]. Dedenbach-Salazar: "-má -mi … -á … convicción profunda, certeza absoluta" (deep conviction, absolute certainty) [verified: 2005: 18]. Yauyos: "pay-ta-m-á rika-ra-ni / he-acc-evd-emph see-pst-1 / 'I saw him.'" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 252, ex. 6]. So in the Ayacucho and Yauyos analyses -má is the direct evidential plus emphatic -á, read as deep conviction; in Faller's Cuzco analysis it is a surprise marker. The two readings are not reconciled in anything opened here. IPA: /ˈma/, /manaˈma/, /qaɾikamaˈma kanqa/.
- **-yá.** Cuzco "emotive": "Qan-puni-yá riki chura-chi-ku-ra-nki / you-def-emo right put-caus-refl-pst-2 / 'Surely, you yourself have designated yourself.'" (Cusihuaman 2001: 244) [verified: Faller 2006: 6, ex. 9]. Ayacucho analyses it as /-ya/ "regretful" plus emphatic -á [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 108; Dedenbach-Salazar 2005: 19]. Yauyos separates "Emphatic -Yá" (*ari-yá* "Yes indeed") from "Emotive -ya", which "indicates regret or resignation … 'alas'" [verified: Shimelman 2017: 252, 269]. IPA: /ˈja/, /qanpuniˈja/, /aɾiˈja/.

## 4. Etymology and family

- **The evidential set is old and opaque.** Bernárdez gives three grounds for its antiquity: "(1) la escasa diferenciación dialectal, (2) la imposibilidad de analizar los sufijos (es decir, las fuentes de una probable gramaticalización no resultan accesibles) y (3) su existencia ya en tiempos de la conquista" (little dialect differentiation; the suffixes cannot be analysed; they already existed at the conquest) [verified: Bernárdez 2020: 36]. My recollection is that Proto-Quechua forms like *-mi, *-ši and a retroflex-initial conjectural (*-ĉa or similar) are reconstructed, but I have not seen the reconstruction in an opened source [from memory: not re-checked]. The spread of conjectural shapes fits a retroflex original: Cuzco/Ayacucho /tʃa/, Wanka /ʈʂa/, Yauyos /ʈʂi/, Huallaga/Ancash /tʃi/. But nothing here is verified as a reconstruction.
- **-chu.** -chu is cognate with Ancash -tsu "negative". I have not opened a reconstruction of it [from memory: not re-checked]. Its doubling into the question enclitic, and into Wanka -chun (-chu + -n), Holguín's -chum and -chuch, and Ayacucho -chusmi, is visible in the sources above.
- **Person and number.** The isomorphic person marks and the two-part inclusive *-n-čik* are discussed in §3.5. Cerrón-Palomino reconstructs pre-PQ person references "*-ya 'primera persona' … *-yki 'segunda persona' … *-n 'tercera persona'" [verified: 1987: 273, page image].
- **Templates.** Ayacucho enclitic order: puni | pas | raq/ña | taq | ču | mI/sI/čA/qa | iki/ya | Á [verified: Solá & Parker 1964: 108]. Dedenbach-Salazar's Cuadro 4 gives the same order, but she takes it from Dedenbach-Salazar et al. 2002, "basado en Parker 1969: 78", the later edition of the same Parker material [verified: 2005: 16–17], so it is not a second witness. The South Conchucos verb template has an "INDEPENDENT" position for evidential enclitics after subject marking [verified: Hintz 2011: 17, Fig. 1.1].
- **Descent and contact.** The varieties in §2 are one family. Their agreements are inheritance. The inclusive/exclusive split may be shared with Aymara by convergence (§3.5). I have not opened anything on whether Quechua's evidential set and Aymara's are related by contact; that is a lead (§9).

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

English speakers reach for hedges and paraphrases where Quechua uses one syllable. "I'd guess", "apparently", "they say" and "it turns out" all stand in for one: "turns out he was Pedro" is Holguín's *Pedrom kasqa*. "I think not" is *manachusmi*. And English needs "we — I mean you and me" against "we — not you" for what Quechua splits into *ñuqanchik* and *ñuqayku*.

Near-misses, each failing:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| English *apparently* | fuses inference and hearsay [from memory: not re-checked]; Quechua keeps -si and -chá/-chus hina apart |
| English *must* / *might* | modal on the proposition. Faller's tests show the Quechua evidentials do not embed and outscope negation, unlike -puni and -man (§3.2) |
| English *it turns out* | carries the surprise of -sqa/-naq but not its non-control, non-experienced reading ("I kissed that girl… or dreamt it") |
| Tok Pisin *yumi* | a visibly compositional inclusive. Quechua *ñuqanchik* is not one: *-chik* marks the addressee-group, with no word for "you" inside (§3.5) |

**Canon-collision check (2026-11-10, against the repo as read):** YOUSPEAK already holds *-mi* (U+E14D), *-si* (U+E14E), *-chu* (U+E14F) and *-auth* (U+E150) as evidential grammar. *debweme* holds experiential truth, "authority comes from having been there", near Faller's BPG. *verisleight* and *veriseem* hold truths deployed or seeming wrongly, the faults the evidentials are used to guard against in ORDO. *aletheme* and *emetme* hold truth as unconcealment and as firmness. None of them holds the conjectural as distinct from the inferential, the non-experienced or mirative past, or the inclusive/exclusive split in its Quechua shape. *yumi:* in grammars/ankyrance/ holds the inclusive anchor, from Tok Pisin.

## 6. Convergence-mapping

- **Existing convergences this file touches:** the evidential organ (grammars/evidentials/) and the clusivity witness list in archaeology/tok-pisin/yumi-and-clusivity.md. That file's line "Grammars that mark how-you-know tend also to mark whom-you-include: both are honesty systems" is not supported by anything opened here. In Quechua the two co-occur, but so they do in Aymara, a contact neighbour (§3.5). Co-occurrence in a contact area is not a typological tendency.
- **New patterns, not yet housed:** (i) the mirative past, Quechua -sqa/-naq; (ii) building inference-hedges out of the question enclitic (-chu with statement intonation; -chus hina; -chusmi); (iii) colonial exclusive "we" as status boundary (Holguín's *viracochamcayco*, *ñocayco padre cuna*). For each I have opened only Quechua sources. Other-family witnesses remain to be opened, and I name none here, so as not to count unopened witnesses.
- **Contact flags:** Quechua–Aymara (two millennia of contact, Cerrón-Palomino 1987: 274). He names Uru-Chipaya as a third Andean family with the inclusive/exclusive split (same page); its contact status was not examined here. The Quechua varieties among themselves are one witness by descent. Within the scholarship, Dedenbach-Salazar's Ayacucho classification descends from Parker's, so it does not corroborate Solá & Parker independently (§2.2).

Convergence cardinality: **1** (the Quechuan family, counted once). No independent second witness was opened in this file.

## 7. Connectable canon-words

- **-mi / -si / -chu / -auth** (grammars/evidentials/, U+E14D–U+E150): this file corrects the donor record of -chu. It also supplies the full donor record of -mi/-si: allomorphs, the BPG and commitment readings, and the fact that they are optional.
- **debweme** (Core): neighbours Faller's BPG. Its "authority from having been there" is close to her "authority over the information".
- **verisleight** (Specialized) and the ORDO verisleight-guard: the guard forbids dressing a -si/-chu value as -mi. Faller's reportative findings (§3.2) support the guard's assumption that a report is not a commitment.
- **aletheme** (Specialized): ORDO's "attending" reports its findings "born -chu". If the language keeps -chu for "inferred", that pairing is unaffected by this file. If it respells, the attending's grade label changes with it.
- **yumi:** (grammars/ankyrance/): Quechua is a clusivity witness, but not a witness for the Law of Visible Constituents (§3.5).

## 9. Leads (not coinages)

- **Lead 1 — conjecture and inference are two grades.** Cuzco separates -chá ("my hunch") from -chus hina ("apparently, from partial evidence"). South Conchucos has several conjecturals. ORDO's lattice has one "inferred" grade. *Weaknesses:* adding a grade adds a codepoint, a glyph and a lattice rule. Faller calls -chus hina's evidential status unsettled. Bendezú-Araujo could not elicit the South Conchucos subdivisions.
- **Lead 2 — a non-experienced or mirative mark from -sqa.** Holguín's *kasqa* and Solá & Parker's "quotative past" name "it turns out / not under my control". *Weaknesses:* -sqa also functions as a nominaliser (Solá & Parker 1964: 59). It is a tense, not an enclitic, so it does not slot into YOUSPEAK's sentence-final evidentials. And it overlaps -si in narrative.
- **Lead 3 — the exclusive "we" as boundary.** Holguín's examples show the colonial exclusive used across religion, race, gender and office. grammars/ankyrance/ "declines to consecrate" the exclusive. These sources support that caution. *Weakness:* one colonial grammarian's examples are not a survey of modern use.
- **Lead 4 — the Bernárdez caution for -auth.** A translation that makes everything -mi, or strips the markers, removes the hearer's ability to weigh a sacred text (§0). *Weakness:* Bernárdez examined two translations, and only evangelical and Mormon ones. He says Catholic texts could not be consulted.
- **Lead 5 — whether Quechua and Aymara evidential sets are related by contact.** This was not opened here and is needed before any convergence count uses both.
