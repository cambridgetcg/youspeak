# Mēʾēn and mēʾūn — "and he refused"

_The accept dig (naaseh-venishma.md) ended on the min's sentence in b. Shabbat 88a, which holds the decline in the same breath as the accept: "if you were able, you would have accepted; and if not, you would not have accepted". This dig follows that "not" into the rest of the Hebrew record. It finds three things. First, the plain refusal verb mēʾēn is, in BDB's words, used "especially of refusing to obey YHWH's commands" (BDB's list of human-subject uses is about as long; "especially" is BDB's emphasis, not a count), and Isaiah sets it against willingness-and-hearing with the sword as its wage: in the covenant register a refusal is a breach. Second, the same verb carries refusals that keep a bond when they are spoken face to face and grounded in the refuser's own standing: Jacob to Joseph, "I know, my son, I know"; Elisha to Naaman, followed by "Go in peace". Third, and this is the finding that matters most, the Mishnah has one legal institution, mēʾūn, in which a refusal is the refuser's own complete act, needs no reason, and is defined by what it does not leave behind: no affinity prohibitions, no disqualification, and the door back to the man she refused still open. The dig also reports what the reception history does to people who decline, and to one who took a no at its word: Orpah becomes the mother of Goliath, the redeemer of Ruth 4 loses his name, and Naomi's thrice-repeated "turn back" is read as a ritual refusal designed to be overridden._

## 1. The concept in compact form

**מֵאֵן mēʾēn** (Piel; no Qal is attested) means "refuse". BDB lists it with a human subject (Gen 37:35, 39:8, 48:19; Ex 22:16; Num 20:21; Dt 25:7; 1 Sam 8:19, 28:23; 2 Kgs 5:16; Esth 1:12; Jer 31:15 and others), "especially of refusing to obey Y's commands" (Ex 4:23, 7:14, 10:3, 16:28; Jer 3:3, 5:3, 9:5; Hos 11:5; Neh 9:17), and once with YHWH as subject (Num 22:13). It adds that in post-biblical Hebrew the Piel is the technical term for a girl's refusing to acknowledge a marriage contract [verified: BDB s.v. מָאֵן, sefaria.org/api/words/מאן]. The noun of that technical sense is **מֵאוּן / מִיאוּן mēʾūn / mīʾūn**, "refusal" (Jastrow s.v. מָאַן: "to protest against, to annul one's marriage") [verified: Jastrow, same lookup].

Its counterpart in willing is **אָבָה ʾābāh**, "be willing, consent". Klein notes that Isa 1:19 and Job 39:9 are "the only passages in the Bible where אָבָה is without the neg. part. לֹא" [verified: Klein s.v. אבה, sefaria.org/api/words/אבה]. So Biblical Hebrew mostly says "willing" in order to say "not willing": *lōʾ ʾābāh*, "he would not".

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, or consciously decline:**

1. **In the covenant register, refusal is breach.** Isa 1:19–20 pairs *ʾim-tōʾbū ū-šəmaʿtem*, "if you are willing and hear", against *wə-ʾim-təmāʾănū ū-mərītem*, "and if you refuse and rebel": the first eats the good of the land, the second is eaten by the sword [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Isaiah.1.18-20]. Here refusing is joined to rebelling. A decline-word drawn from mēʾēn carries this.
2. **In narrative, the same verb carries refusals that keep a bond.** They are spoken face to face, they give the refuser's own ground rather than an excuse, and they are followed by a continuing relation (Jacob, Gen 48:19; Elisha, 2 Kgs 5:16, 19). Others break it (Vashti, Esth 1:12–19).
3. **In law, mēʾūn is the refuser's act, needs no reason, and leaves no residue.** Shmuel: "she acts on him", by saying "I do not desire you, I do not want you" (b. Yevamot 107b; said of a minor widow refusing one levir, and disputed there by Rav, §3.7). The Mishnah defines it by contrast with divorce: after mēʾūn the man may marry her relatives, she may marry his, she is not disqualified from marrying a priest, and if she later leaves another husband she may return to him; after a *gēṭ* none of this holds (m. Yevamot 13:4). It is the only case found in this dig where a tradition defines a refusal by the door it leaves open.
4. **The reception history is hard on the people who decline, and on one who took a no at its word.** Orpah, who turned back when asked, is made the mother of Goliath (b. Sotah 42b). The redeemer who declined in Ruth 4 is called "So-and-so" and, in one reading, "mute in Torah" (Ruth Rabbah 7:7). And Naomi's three "turn back"s are read as the three times one pushes away a convert, to be overcome by insistence (Ruth Rabbah 2:16). The reception knows the ritual refusal that is meant to be overridden, and b. Sotah 42b disparages Orpah, who took it at its word, though Rava in the same passage also speaks of a reward for her tears (§3.5).

**Sibling distinctions.** Three words, three registers:
- **mēʾēn** is the act of refusing, aimed at a command, a request, a gift or a comfort. In the prophets it is rebellion; in narrative it can be grief, integrity or tenderness.
- **lōʾ ʾābāh** is the absence of will: "he would not". It names the refusal as a lack, the failure of a yes. That is the opposite of the realm feature, which wants the no as a complete act.
- **mēʾūn** is the legal act: a declaration that dissolves a bond made for the refuser without her consent, and leaves nothing behind but an open door.

## 3. Primary attestations, each read to its end

### 3.1 Isaiah 1:18–20 — the covenant pair: willing-and-hearing against refusing-and-rebelling

> אִם־תֹּאבוּ וּשְׁמַעְתֶּם טוּב הָאָרֶץ תֹּאכֵלוּ׃ וְאִם־תְּמָאֲנוּ וּמְרִיתֶם חֶרֶב תְּאֻכְּלוּ כִּי פִּי יְהֹוָה דִּבֵּר׃
> "If you are willing and you hear, you shall eat the good of the land; and if you refuse and rebel, you shall be eaten by the sword; for the mouth of YHWH has spoken." (translation mine, literal)
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Isaiah.1.18-20; JPS 2023 has "If, then, you agree and give heed … But if you refuse and disobey", with a note that "you will be devoured [by] the sword" may be "you will be fed the sword"]

The pairing is explicit and symmetrical. *ʾābāh* + *šāmaʿ* (the accept side, and the same verb of hearing as *nišmāʿ*) stands against *mēʾēn* + *mārāh*. **Relation:** the refusal breaks it. **Pairing with acceptance:** yes, in one verse.

### 3.2 Genesis 48:17–20 — "I know, my son, I know"

Joseph sees Jacob's right hand on the younger Ephraim, tries to move it, and says "Not so, father, for this is the firstborn". Then:

> וַיְמָאֵן אָבִיו וַיֹּאמֶר יָדַעְתִּי בְנִי יָדַעְתִּי גַּם־הוּא יִהְיֶה־לְּעָם וְגַם־הוּא יִגְדָּל וְאוּלָם אָחִיו הַקָּטֹן יִגְדַּל מִמֶּנּוּ וְזַרְעוֹ יִהְיֶה מְלֹא־הַגּוֹיִם
> "And his father refused, and said: I know, my son, I know. He too shall become a people, and he too shall be great; but his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his seed shall become a multitude of nations."
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Genesis.48.17-20; translation mine]

The chapter goes on: "So he blessed them that day … and he set Ephraim before Manasseh" (48:20). The refusal is spoken to Joseph by name ("my son"), acknowledges his objection twice ("I know … I know"), gives the elder son a blessing of his own, and then restates exactly what Joseph objected to: the younger shall be greater. The refusal does not soften its content. It is followed by a blessing of both. **Relation:** kept. Joseph's request is declined; nothing in the text breaks between them. **Note:** this is a refusal of a correction, not of an offer. It is the clearest case found in this dig of a refusal that names the bond while refusing.

### 3.3 2 Kings 5:15–27 — Elisha declines Naaman's gift, says "Go in peace", and Gehazi undoes it

> וַיֹּאמֶר חַי־יְהֹוָה אֲשֶׁר־עָמַדְתִּי לְפָנָיו אִם־אֶקָּח וַיִּפְצַר־בּוֹ לָקַחַת וַיְמָאֵן
> "And he said: As YHWH lives, before whom I stand, I will not take. And he urged him to take, and he refused." (5:16)
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/II_Kings.5.15-19; translation mine]

Naaman then asks two things: earth to carry home, and pardon in advance for bowing in the temple of Rimmon when his master leans on his arm. Elisha answers only *lēkh lə-šālōm*, "Go in peace" (5:19) [verified]. Read to its end, the chapter turns. Gehazi, Elisha's servant, says to himself that his master "has let that Aramean Naaman off without accepting what he brought" (JPS 2023's English; literally "has spared Naaman … from taking from his hand what he brought"), runs after him, takes two talents of silver and two changes of clothes by a lie, and is struck with Naaman's leprosy (5:20–27) [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/II_Kings.5.20-27].

**Relation:** kept, and widened. The refusal is grounded in the refuser's own standing ("before whom I stand"), not in a judgment of the giver. It is repeated under pressure, and it is followed by a blessing that releases the other ("go in peace"). **The end of the chapter matters:** the refusal is undone by a third party's acceptance on the refuser's behalf, and the text treats that acceptance as the breach.

### 3.4 Esther 1:10–22 — Vashti's refusal, and the edict that follows it

> וַתְּמָאֵן הַמַּלְכָּה וַשְׁתִּי לָבוֹא בִּדְבַר הַמֶּלֶךְ … וַיִּקְצֹף הַמֶּלֶךְ מְאֹד
> "And Queen Vashti refused to come at the king's word … and the king was very angry." (1:12)
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Esther.1.10-22]

Read to the end of the chapter: Memucan argues that her refusal will make all wives despise their husbands (1:16–18); an irrevocable edict decrees "that Vashti shall never enter the presence of King Ahasuerus", and her royal state is given to another (1:19); letters go out that every man shall rule in his house (1:22) [verified]. **Relation:** broken, by the one refused. Vashti's refusal is a sovereign act that the text does not condemn in its own voice (her reason is never given), but the bond does not survive it. **This is the counter-case** a decline-word must be able to name: the no that the other side answers with rupture.

### 3.5 Ruth 1:8–18 and its reception — Orpah, who believed the "turn back"

Naomi tells both daughters-in-law three times to turn back (*šōbnāh*, 1:8, 1:11, 1:12). Both first refuse: "No, we will return with you to your people" (*kī-ʾittākh nāšūb*, 1:10). After the third, "Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, and Ruth clung to her" (1:14). Naomi: "See, your sister-in-law has returned to her people and her gods; turn back (*šūbī*) after your sister-in-law" (1:15): a fourth urging, in the singular, to Ruth alone, which Ruth Rabbah's count of three (below) does not include. Ruth: *ʾal-tifgəʿī-vī*, "do not press me to leave you" (1:16). "When she saw that she was determined to go with her, she ceased to speak to her" (1:18) [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Ruth.1.8-18, Ruth.1.8, 1.10, 1.14-16, 1.18; translations mine].

Two things follow for the decline target.
- **Orpah's turning back is not a refusal of Naomi. It is acceptance of Naomi's request.** The refusal in the chapter is Ruth's: she refuses Naomi's urging, and that refusal is what keeps the bond. The lead "Orpah" (uptake-convergence-notes.md D3) is therefore mislabelled: the decline that keeps the bond here is Ruth's "do not press me".
- **The reception reads Naomi's urging as a ritual refusal.** Ruth Rabbah 2:16, R. Shmuel bar Naḥmani in the name of R. Yudan bar R. Ḥanina: "In three places 'turn back' is written here (Ruth 1:8, 1:11, 1:12), corresponding to the three times that one pushes away a convert (*šeddōḥīn ʾet ha-gēr*); if he insists beyond that, one accepts him (*məqabbəlīn ʾōtō*). R. Yitzḥak said: 'The stranger shall not lodge outside' (Job 31:32); a person should always push away with the left and draw near with the right" [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Ruth_Rabbah.2.16; translation mine, after the Sefaria English]. R. Yitzḥak's proof-text sets a limit on the pushing away: the stranger is not to be left outside. On this reading Naomi's no was a test, not meant to be the last word, and the accepting verb is q-b-l, the accept dig's root.

And the reception of Orpah is hostile. b. Sotah 42b identifies Orpah with Harafah, mother of the four Philistine giants (2 Sam 21:22), with sexually degrading etymologies of both names. R. Yitzḥak: "Let the children of the one who kissed come and fall into the hands of the children of the one who clung." Rava: "As a reward for the four tears that Orpah shed over her mother-in-law, she merited that four mighty men came from her" [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Sotah.42b, segments 6–8].

**Relation:** Orpah's farewell keeps the bond in the text (a kiss, weeping). The tradition does not keep it with her. **Polite refusal not free:** Naomi's thrice-spoken "turn back" is, in Ruth Rabbah, a refusal designed to be overcome, and the person who obeyed it is the one later disparaged. That is the most dangerous way this gap can be flattered: by a reading in which a "no" was a test, and believing it is a failure.

### 3.6 Ruth 4:1–10 against Deuteronomy 25:7–10 — the invited decline and the shamed one

Boaz to the nearer redeemer, before ten elders:

> אִם־תִּגְאַל גְּאָל וְאִם־לֹא יִגְאַל הַגִּידָה לִּי וְאֵדְעָה
> "If you will redeem, redeem; and if not, tell me, that I may know." (4:4)

The redeemer first says "I will redeem". Told that Ruth comes with the field, he says:

> לֹא אוּכַל לִגְאָל־לִי פֶּן־אַשְׁחִית אֶת־נַחֲלָתִי גְּאַל־לְךָ אַתָּה אֶת־גְּאֻלָּתִי כִּי לֹא־אוּכַל לִגְאֹל
> "I cannot redeem for myself, lest I ruin my own inheritance. Redeem, you, for yourself my right of redemption, for I cannot redeem." (4:6)

Then the custom: "formerly in Israel, concerning redemption and exchange, to confirm any matter, a man drew off (*šālap̄*) his sandal and gave it to his fellow" (4:7). In 4:8 the redeemer says to Boaz "acquire for yourself", "and he drew off his sandal". The verse does not name who drew it off or say that it was handed over; the giving is supplied by the custom of 4:7 [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Ruth.4.1-10, 4.4, 4.6-7; ketiv/qere at 4:4 and 4:6 as printed there]. The custom is a general seal on transfers of right ("redemption and exchange"), not a rite of declining.

Set beside Dt 25:7–10, the law for a brother-in-law who refuses levirate marriage (*mēʾēn yəbāmī*, "my brother-in-law refuses", 25:7): the widow draws off his sandal before the elders, spits in his face, and declares "So shall be done to the man who will not build up his brother's house"; his house is called "the house of the one whose sandal was drawn off" (25:9–10) [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Deuteronomy.25.7-10].

So the same Hebrew legal world holds two forms of declining a duty of kinship:
- In Deuteronomy the refuser is permitted to refuse, and is shamed.
- In Ruth the decline is invited as an equal option ("if not, tell me, that I may know"), given with a reason, completed by handing the right to the next person ("redeem, you, for yourself"), and sealed by the transfer custom: the sandal is drawn off (*šālap̄*), on the usual reading by the redeemer himself, not pulled off him by the widow (*ḥālaṣ*, Dt 25:9). Nobody spits.

**Relation:** kept with Boaz, and the decline makes the book's ending possible. **But the reception does not let it rest.** The text never names him: Boaz calls him *pəlōnī ʾalmōnī*, "So-and-so" (4:1). Ruth Rabbah 7:7 records R. Yehoshua's view that this was his name, and R. Shmuel bar Naḥman's that he was *ʾillēm*, "mute", in words of Torah: he declined because "the first ones died only because they took them [Moabite wives]", and he feared Ruth the Moabite would taint his children, "not knowing that the law had already been renewed: an Ammonite, not an Ammonitess; a Moabite, not a Moabitess" [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Ruth_Rabbah.7.7, repeated at 7.10]. The decline is remembered as ignorance.

### 3.7 m. Yevamot 13:1, 13:4 and b. Yevamot 107b–108a — mēʾūn: the refusal that leaves the way back open

**The institution.** A girl whose father has died may be married off as a minor by her mother or brothers. Such a marriage is rabbinic, and she may annul it by refusal (*mēʾūn*) before majority (the framing is the Davidson edition's explanatory English to m. Yev 13:1, which I take as context, not as the Mishnah's words) [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Mishnah_Yevamot.13.1].

**m. Yevamot 13:1.** Beit Shammai: only betrothed girls refuse; in his presence; in court. Beit Hillel: betrothed and married; in his presence or not; in court or not. Beit Hillel: "she may refuse while a minor, even four or five times". Beit Shammai: "the daughters of Israel are not ownerless (*hefqēr*); rather she refuses and waits until she grows up, and refuses and marries" [verified; translation mine].

**m. Yevamot 13:4 — the definition by residue:**

> הַמְמָאֶנֶת בָּאִישׁ, הוּא מֻתָּר בִּקְרוֹבוֹתֶיהָ, וְהִיא מֻתֶּרֶת בִּקְרוֹבָיו, וְלֹא פְסָלָהּ מִן הַכְּהֻנָּה. נָתַן לָהּ גֵּט, הוּא אָסוּר בִּקְרוֹבוֹתֶיהָ, וְהִיא אֲסוּרָה בִקְרוֹבָיו, וּפְסָלָהּ מִן הַכְּהֻנָּה … זֶה הַכְּלָל, גֵּט אַחַר מֵאוּן, אֲסוּרָה לַחֲזֹר לוֹ. מֵאוּן אַחַר גֵּט, מֻתֶּרֶת לַחֲזֹר לוֹ.
> "She who refuses a man: he is permitted her relatives, and she is permitted his relatives, and he has not disqualified her from the priesthood. If he gave her a bill of divorce: he is forbidden her relatives, she is forbidden his relatives, and he has disqualified her from the priesthood … This is the rule: a divorce after a refusal, she is forbidden to return to him; a refusal after a divorce, she is permitted to return to him."
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Mishnah_Yevamot.13.4; translation mine]

**b. Yevamot 107b — whose act it is.** The case is narrower than mēʾūn in general: a minor widow who falls to her late husband's brothers for levirate marriage refuses one of them (a *yāvām*). Rav: she is then forbidden to the other brother too, as with a bill of divorce. Shmuel: "If she refused this *yāvām*, she is permitted to that one; it is not like a woman with a bill of divorce. With a bill of divorce, *he* acts on her. Here *she* acts on him (*hī qāʿāvdā bēh*), for she says: 'I do not desire you and I do not want you'. It is you I do not desire; your fellow I do desire" [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Yevamot.107b, segments 3–4; translation mine]. Rav Asi then holds that she may even return to the one she refused (segment 5). "She acts on him" is Shmuel's side of a dispute, said of a levir.

**b. Yevamot 108a — the formula and its minimum.** "What is a refusal? If she said: 'I do not want so-and-so my husband', 'I do not want the betrothal my mother and brothers made for me'." R. Yehuda: even if she says it in the bridal litter on the way to his house. R. Yosei bar R. Yehuda: even if he sent her to a shopkeeper for something of his and she said it, "there is no greater refusal than this". The earlier written form, "I do not desire him, I do not want him, I do not wish to be married to him", was changed because, being long, it "may come to be confused with a bill of divorce"; the later form records only "On such a day, so-and-so daughter of so-and-so refused before us" [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Yevamot.107b, segment 17; Yevamot.108a, segments 0–2]. The reason given is to keep the refusal from being mistaken for a divorce.

What this gives the forge:
- **The refusal is a complete act of the refuser.** Shmuel sets it against divorce precisely on who acts (in the levirate case, and against Rav).
- **It needs no reason.** The formula is "I do not want", nothing more, and the Gemara treats the bare words, said anywhere, as sufficient.
- **It is defined by what it does not leave.** No affinity, no disqualification, and the way back to him stays open.
- **It is not "keeping the bond" in the plain sense.** The marriage ends. What stays whole is the refuser's standing and the possibility of relation. That is narrower than "leaves the table set", and it must be said so.
- **It is gendered and age-bound.** It protects a minor girl from a marriage made for her. Beit Shammai's "the daughters of Israel are not ownerless" is the reason the institution exists. A coinage that generalises it carries that history.

**Pairing with acceptance:** here refusal is set against divorce (*gēṭ*), not against acceptance. That is itself a finding: Mishnaic law distinguishes two ways out of a bond, and only one of them is a refusal.

### 3.8 b. Shabbat 88a.9 — the min's "and if not"

> בְּרֵישָׁא אִיבַּעְיָא לְכוּ לְמִשְׁמַע, אִי מָצִיתוּ — קַבְּלִיתוּ, וְאִי לָא — לָא קַבְּלִיתוּ
> "First you ought to have listened; if you were able, you would have accepted; and if not, you would not have accepted."
> [verified: sefaria.org/api/texts/Shabbat.88a.9; the Davidson English turns the perfects into imperatives, "accept them … do not accept them"; see naaseh-venishma.md §3.3(e)]

The decline is held as an equal outcome of a prior hearing, in a sectarian's mouth, and Rava answers with the integrity of the relationship, not by denying the option: "We who walk in wholeness, of us it is written 'The integrity of the upright shall guide them' (Prov 11:3)" [verified: Rava's answer begins at the end of Shabbat 88a:9 and runs on in sefaria.org/api/texts/Shabbat.88b.1]. **Pairing:** *qabbəlītū / lā qabbəlītū*, accept / not-accept: the decline is named as the negation of the accept verb, which is exactly what the realm feature ("not the failure of a yes") resists.

## 4. Etymology and family

- **m-ʾ-n.** BDB: Piel "refuse"; "Qal perhaps *be distasteful*"; cf. Syriac ܡܶܐܢ "taeduit, piguit" (was weary, was loath), Aphel "be slothful"; "poss. akin to Ethiopic መነነ reject"; Arabic مَأَنَ is "sustain, maintain" (a different sense) [verified: BDB s.v. מָאֵן]. Ben Yehuda's dictionary suggests it may be a metathesis of מנע, "withhold", with the ʿayin softened to aleph [verified: Ben Yehuda s.v. מאן, same Sefaria lookup; a suggestion, not a consensus].
- **ʾ-b-y.** BDB s.v. אָבָה: "be willing, consent (cf. Assyrian *abîtu*, desire … Ethiopic አበየ refuse, Arabic أَبَى id. [refuse] … Nejd *be willing*)" [verified]. Wiktionary's Arabic entry records a theory that the root's first meaning was "want", that it "came to be always used with negation, such that this is the bulk of attestation of [Hebrew] אָבָה", and that standard Arabic then dropped the negator, so that the bare verb now means "refuse" [verified: en.wiktionary.org/wiki/أبى, Arabic, Etymology 1; a theory reported there, not checked in a primary Semitist source]. The same entry calls the derivation "supposedly" from Proto-Semitic and records an alternative view, which explains the non-standard "want" senses from a mutilated *baghā* "to desire" [verified: same entry]. On the first theory, Semitic "refuse" is a fossilised "not want": the failure of a yes, which is what the realm feature does not want.
- **Morphology.** *mēʾūn* is a Piel verbal noun of the *qittūl* pattern (cf. *qibbūl*, "acceptance") [pattern identification mine; not checked against a grammar]. If that holds, the decline noun and the accept noun of Mishnaic Hebrew are formed alike.
- **Doctrinal family.** *mēʾēn* (refuse) · *lōʾ ʾābāh* (be unwilling) · *mēʾūn* (legal refusal) · *gēṭ* (divorce, the other way out) · *ḥălīṣāh* (the shamed refusal of the levir) · *qibbēl* (accept; naaseh-venishma.md).

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

What the Hebrew record shows people doing, which English names only with a phrase:
- Saying no to a gift because of who you are before God, and then blessing the giver on his way (Elisha).
- Saying no to someone you love while saying, twice, that you have heard them (Jacob).
- Declining a duty and handing it, with a token, to the one who will take it (the redeemer and the sandal).
- Refusing in a way that closes nothing: the person refused may still marry your sister, and you may still come back (mēʾūn).

Near-misses, each failing:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| English *refuse* | carries rejection of the thing and, often, of the person; no built-in "the door stays open" |
| English *decline* | close in politeness; carries no act-sense (one "declines" by omission as easily as by speech) and no relation-sense |
| English *pass* ("I'll pass") | casual; can be the end of a relation as easily as not |
| *lōʾ ʾābāh* | names unwillingness, a lack: the failure of a yes |
| Arabic *ʾabā* | a witness, not a substitute: same root family as *ʾābāh*, and the verb of Iblīs's refusal (archaeology/arabic/iba-itidhar.md) |
| Latin *excusare* | a witness: the decline as a plea with a cause (archaeology/latin/excusare-recusare.md) |

**Canon collision check of the region (2026-11-10, worktree at 621977a):**
- **sinbyeongme** (canon/core/sinbyeongme.md) names the sacred calling that arrives "before the person has agreed to be called"; its body says "A god who asks permission can be refused. Sinbyeongme names a god who does not ask", and cites Moses at the bush as reluctance, not disobedience (line 68). It holds the un-refusable call. The target needs the refusable one.
- **walkekin** (canon/core/walkekin.md) holds "the bond whose substance is demonstrated precisely by the long gap it tolerates". It is the bond's side; it has no act of refusing.

## 8. Forge-targets identified — leads only

House note, as in naaseh-venishma.md §8: this dig is excavation only. No lemma, no IPA of a coinage, no family assignment, no score.

- **Standing target:** wire-task-decline (queued). Realm feature: "sovereign refusal without rupture — 'not mine, not now' that leaves the table set; rest-as-a-whole-beat spoken to someone".
- **Lead H-D1 — mēʾūn / mīʾūn (מֵאוּן).**
  - Carries: the refusal as the refuser's complete act ("she acts on him"); no reason required ("I do not want"); defined by leaving no residue and an open way back (m. Yev 13:4).
  - Weaknesses:
    - It ends the relation it refuses. What it keeps open is standing and possibility, not the bond. "Leaves the table set" fits; "the relation whole" does not, quite.
    - A living halakhic term for a gendered protection of minors. Generalising it needs custodial care (§0) and must say what it drops.
    - Romanization varies (mēʾūn, mīʾūn, miʾun, me'un); the medial aleph is lost on English readers.
    - Its first syllable is the canon's suffix *me* (§9).
- **Lead H-D2 — the verb mēʾēn (מֵאֵן).**
  - Carries: the plain act of refusing, with a human subject, in the narratives where the bond is kept (Gen 48:19; 2 Kgs 5:16).
  - Weaknesses: BDB's "especially of refusing to obey Y's commands"; Isa 1:20 pairs it with rebellion and the sword. The register is breach. Romanized *meʾen* or *mean*, it meets the English word *mean*.
- **Lead H-D3 — ʾābāh / lōʾ ʾābāh (אָבָה).**
  - Carries: the paired verb of willing (Isa 1:19), so a decline-word would stand against an accept-word.
  - Weaknesses: names the no as absence of will, the failure of a yes, the exact thing the realm feature excludes. Its Arabic cognate means "refuse" and is Iblīs's verb. *ʾābāh* sits inside *ʾahăvāh* (canon's ahavame donor) for the ear.
- **Lead H-D4 — the Ruth 4 exchange: *wə-ʾim-lōʾ … haggīdāh lī* / *gəʾal-ləkhā ʾattāh*.**
  - Carries: the decline invited as an equal option, given with a reason, and completed by handing the right to the next person, sealed by the transfer custom of the sandal drawn off (4:7–8), not pulled off by another as in Dt 25:9. This is the closest Hebrew match to "not mine, not now" said to someone who matters.
  - Weaknesses: a scene, not a lexeme; the decliner loses his name and is called "mute in Torah" in Ruth Rabbah 7:7; *gəʾullāh* is redemption, a heavy theological register.
- **Lead H-D5 — *yādaʿtī bənī yādaʿtī* (Gen 48:19).**
  - Carries: the tenderness of a refusal that names the bond while refusing.
  - Weaknesses: a narrative instance; it refuses a correction, not an offer; a phrase.

A second Claude subagent re-checked this file as a sceptical reader a few hours later, in the early hours of 2026-11-11 (GMT), with web access (Sefaria API texts and lexicon, en.wiktionary raw text, a grep of canon/). It is a check, not a sign-off.

**Corrected:**
- b. Yevamot 107b: Shmuel's "she acts on him" is said of a minor widow refusing one levir, and is one side of a dispute with Rav (and Rav Asi). The file had presented it as a general definition of mēʾūn, with the levirate context and the dispute left out (§1, §2, §3.7, epigraph).
- b. Yevamot 107b–108a: the long bill of refusal was changed because it "may come to be confused with a bill of divorce", not only because it was long.
- Gen 48:19 was cut before "but his younger brother shall be greater than he": the refusal's own content. Restored. "Clearest case in the Hebrew Bible" narrowed to "in this dig".
- Ruth 1:15 holds a fourth urging (*šūbī*, to Ruth alone) that the file omitted.
- Ruth Rabbah 2:16: attribution added (R. Shmuel bar Naḥmani for R. Yudan bar R. Ḥanina), and R. Yitzḥak's proof-text Job 31:32, "the stranger shall not lodge outside", restored where the file had an ellipsis. It limits the "push away" reading.
- Ruth Rabbah 7:7: the redeemer's stated fear includes death ("the first ones died only because they took them"), not only taint.
- Ruth 4:7–8: the custom concerns "redemption and exchange"; 4:8 says only "he drew off his sandal" (agent unnamed, giving not stated). "A sandal given, not pulled off" is replaced by the verbs the text uses (*šālap̄* against Dt 25:9 *ḥālaṣ*).
- Rava's answer in b. Shabbat is at 88b:1, not in 88a:9 as the tag said.
- "By BDB's own count" was not a count; now "in BDB's words".
- Jer 31:15 *kī ʾēnennū* is singular. The 2 Kgs 5:20 quotation is marked as JPS's English. Gen 39: the bond with Potiphar is lost, not kept.
- Wiktionary's alternative view of ʾ-b-y is now reported beside the negation theory.
- §9: three canon headwords begin with *me-* (metastrophesis was missed).
