# 辭 cí and 讓 ràng — "I dare decline"

_The accept dig found that Classical Chinese separates the prompt deferential "yes" (唯) from the deliberate consenting "yes" (諾). This file opens the other side. The character 辭 covers words and speech, the statements of a lawsuit, declining, thanking and taking one's leave; its variant 辤 is defined as "not accepting" and is written with the very character for accepting (受) inside it. Its partner 讓 began as "reproach" and became "yielding": declining what one ought to receive and pushing it on to another. The ritual texts show the hardest truth for this gap: the commonest decline in the ritual classics is a scripted stage of consent. The guest asked to officiate at a capping says "I dare decline", is asked again, and says "how dare I not follow?"; and the young may not decline what an elder gives. Against that, Mengzi argues case by case for refusals that are real, and Zhuangzi gives the purest "not mine" in the record: a hermit offered the empire who tells the sage-king to go home and rest._

## 1. The concept in compact form

**辭 cí.** Kangxi s.v. 辭: read 似兹切 (Tangyun); "辭說也", speech, statement (with Yijing, Qian: 修辭立其誠, "cultivating one's words establishes one's sincerity"); "又《說文》訟辭也", the statements in a lawsuit (with Zhouli: of the five ways of hearing a case, "the first is hearing the statements", 辭聽; and Shu, "Lü xing": 單辭 "one party's statement" against 兩辭 "both parties' statements"); "又與辤同。《正韻》卻不受也", the same as 辤, "to refuse, not accept" (with Shu, "Da Yu mo": 稽首固辭, "bowed his head to the ground and firmly declined"; and Zhongyong: 爵祿可辭也, "rank and salary can be declined"); "又謝也", the same as 謝 (Kangxi's only example is 溫顏遜辭 from the Han shu biography of Han Wang Xin, "gentle face and humble words"; which sense of 謝, thanking, declining or apologising, is meant is not stated); "又別去也", to take one's leave (with Chuci, "Jiu ge": 入不言兮出不辭, "he came in without a word and went out without taking leave") [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 康熙字典/辛部/十二畫, raw text; glosses mine].

**辤 cí** (variant). Kangxi: "同辭。《說文》不受也。从辛从受。受卽爲辛，宜辤之也", "same as 辭. Shuowen: not accepting. From 辛 [bitter] and 受 [receive]: to receive it would be bitter, so it is right to decline it" [verified: 康熙字典/辛部/八畫; the Shuowen itself not opened]. **The refusal character is written with the acceptance character inside it**, on the Shuowen's analysis. The same Kangxi entry goes on: "又與文辭之辭通用", it is also used for 辭 in the sense "words" (with 絕妙好辤 from the Wei zhi), and Unihan's definition of 辤 is "words, speech; a sentence, an expression or phrase; a message" [verified: same entry; Unihan U+8FA4]. In use the graph is not reserved for refusing. 受 shòu is defined in Unihan as "receive, accept, get; bear, stand" [verified: Unihan U+53D7].

**讓 ràng.** Kangxi: "《說文》相責讓也", mutual reproach; "《小爾雅》詰責以辭謂之讓", "to rebuke with words is called 讓"; then "《玉篇》謙也", modesty; "《類篇》退也", withdrawing; "《字彙》先人後己謂之讓", "putting others first and oneself after is called 讓"; with Shu, "Yao dian": 允恭克讓; Zuozhuan Wen 1: 卑讓，德之基也, "humble yielding is the foundation of virtue"; Xiang 13: 讓，禮之主也, "yielding is the master of ritual". Two glosses define it against neighbours: the Liji "Qu li" subcommentary (疏): **應受而推曰讓**, "to push away what one ought to receive is called 讓"; and the note on the Yili "Xiang yin jiu li": **事同曰讓，事異曰辭**, "when the matter is the same it is called 讓; when the matter differs it is called 辭" [verified: 康熙字典/言部/十七畫; the commentaries quoted there were not opened directly]. Unihan's modern definition is "allow, permit, yield, concede" [verified: Unihan U+8B93].

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, or consciously decline:**

1. **To decline is to speak, and to take one's leave.** One character holds words, a party's plea, refusal, thanks and departure. The image of its absence is the Chuci line: coming in without speaking, leaving without 辭. In this language a decline is a leave-taking that is said.
2. **The refusal is written as not-receiving.** 辤 = 辛 + 受, glossed 不受. The language's decline is the negation of its accept: the shape the realm feature calls "the failure of a yes".
3. **The commonest decline is a stage of consent.** The Yili scripts it (*賓禮辭，許*: "the guest declines as ritual requires, then consents"), and the Liji makes it compulsory before answering an elder and forbidden before an elder's gift (§3.1–3.2).
4. **Real refusals are argued, case by case.** Mengzi refuses a king's gold where there was no ground for it (2B3), discusses whether refusing a superior's gift is disrespect (5B4), and takes Zisi's side when he refuses a ruler's gifts whose form kept him bowing like a servant (5B6). Zhuangzi's Xu You declines the empire because it is not his to take (§3.4).
5. **讓 is yielding in another's favour.** It is "not mine; yours": the decliner pushes on to another what was his due (應受而推). It is also, in Zhuangzi, the word for Yao's offer: the giver "yields" the realm. Offer and decline share one verb.

**Sibling distinctions.**
- **辭** declines with words and goes; its object can be anything.
- **讓** declines what is one's own due, in favour of another; "事同曰讓": the same thing passes on.
- **卻** pushes a gift back; it is the gesture in Mengzi's debate about disrespect.
- **諾 / 唯** (uptake-convergence-notes.md) are the two yeses; **許** is consent, the word that ends a ritual 辭.

## 3. Primary attestations, each read to its end

### 3.1 Yili, "Shi guan li" — the scripted decline

The main text: 主人戒賓。賓禮辭，許。主人再拜，賓荅拜, "The host invites the sponsor. The sponsor declines as ritual requires, and consents. The host bows twice; the sponsor bows in return." [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 儀禮/士冠禮, raw text; translation mine].

The appended formulae give the words:

> 戒賓，曰：「某有子某。將加布於其首，願吾子之教之也。」賓對曰：「某不敏，恐不能共事，以病吾子，敢辭。」主人曰：「某猶願吾子之終教之也！」賓對曰：「吾子重有命，某敢不從？」
> "Inviting the sponsor, he says: 'I have a son, so-and-so. We are about to set the cloth cap on his head; I wish you would instruct him.' The sponsor answers: 'I am not quick; I fear I cannot share the task and would trouble you. I dare decline.' The host: 'I still wish that you will in the end instruct him!' The sponsor: 'You lay your command on me twice over; how dare I not follow?'"
> [verified: same page; translation mine]

**The decline is the second line of a four-line acceptance.** It is humble, specific ("I am not quick"), spoken to the person, and meant to be answered by a second request. **Relation:** strengthened, by a refusal that everyone knows is not one.

Zheng Xuan's note on 賓禮辭，許 grades such refusals: 「禮辭，一辭而許。再辭而許曰固辭。三辭曰終辭，不許也。」 "禮辭 is to decline once and then consent. To decline twice and then consent is called 固辭. To decline three times is called 終辭: not consenting." [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 儀禮註疏/卷一, Zheng's note to 主人戒賓，賓禮辭，許; the same note is printed in 儀禮 (四部叢刊本)/卷第一; translation mine]. Jia Gongyan's subcommentary (疏) on the same note adds three things [verified: same page]:
- The count and the outcome both matter: "if one declines three times and then consents, it is called 三辭; only if one declines three times and does not consent is it called 終辭".
- Its example of 終辭 is the Yili "Shi xiang jian li": 士見於大夫，終辭其摯, "when a common officer visits a great officer, [the great officer] finally declines his gift". Opened: the text goes on 於其入也，一拜其辱也。賓退，送，再拜, "when he comes in, [the great officer] bows once to acknowledge the honour of the visit; when the guest withdraws, he sees him off and bows twice" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 儀禮/士相見禮; translation mine]. The gift is refused for good; the visitor is received and seen off with bows.
- On why the sponsor here declines only once, Jia cites Zheng's note to the parallel in "Xiang yin jiu li": 不固辭者，素所有志, "he does not decline firmly because this has long been his wish". The commentary itself reads the single decline as a sign of a yes already held.

So the ritual language has a word for the decline that is not a stage of consent. But it is reached only as the third turn of the same script, and a decline is known to be 終辭 only by its outcome.

### 3.2 Liji, "Qu li shang" — when declining is required, and when it is forbidden

[verified: zh.wikisource.org, 禮記/曲禮上, raw text; translations mine]
- 長者問，不辭讓而對，非禮也, "when an elder asks, to answer without first declining and yielding is against ritual."
- 客至於寢門，則主人請入爲席，然後出迎客；客固辭，主人肅客而入, "… the guest firmly declines; the host bows the guest in."
- 客若降等，則就主人之階；主人固辭，然後客復就西階, "if the guest is of lower rank, he goes to the host's steps; the host firmly declines, and then the guest returns to the west steps."
- 長者賜，少者賤者不敢辭, **"when an elder bestows, the young and the lowly do not dare decline."**
- 御同於長者，雖貳不辭，偶坐不辭, "serving food with an elder, though it be a second helping, one does not decline; sitting as a pair, one does not decline."

So in the same chapter the decline is **required** (before answering an elder), **choreographed** (the host's and the guest's 固辭 over who goes first), and **forbidden** (before an elder's gift). **Not free, said plainly:** this is courtesy-refusal under hierarchy. Its function is to keep the bond by not taking anything too quickly; its value as a "no" is nil, and where the elder gives, even that is withdrawn.

### 3.3 Mengzi — real refusals, argued

**2A6:** 無辭讓之心，非人也 … 辭讓之心，禮之端也, "one without a heart of declining-and-yielding is not human … the heart of declining-and-yielding is the sprout of ritual propriety" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 孟子/公孫丑上]. Declining is placed among the four sprouts of human nature.

**2B3 — the refused gold.** Chen Zhen asks: in Qi you refused a hundred *jin* of fine gold from the king; in Song you accepted seventy *yi*; in Xue fifty. "If the earlier refusal was right, the later acceptance was wrong …". Mengzi: 皆是也, "all were right". In Song he was setting out on a journey and the gift was named a parting present; in Xue he had cause for caution and the gift was named for his guard. 若於齊則未有處也。無處而饋之，是貨之也；焉有君子而可以貨取乎？ "As for Qi, there was no ground for it. To give a gift without a ground is to buy a man; where is the gentleman who can be taken with money?" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 孟子/公孫丑下; translation mine].
- **Relation:** kept, and protected. The refusal keeps the relation from becoming a purchase. Accept and decline are judged by the same rule, so both can be right.

**2B5:** 有官守者，不得其職則去；有言責者，不得其言則去 … 我無官守，我無言責也，則吾進退豈不綽綽然有餘裕哉？ "One who holds an office leaves if he cannot do his duty; one with a duty to speak leaves if his words are not taken … I have no office to keep and no duty to speak: in advancing and withdrawing, have I not ample room?" [verified: same page]. Freedom to withdraw is grounded in having no charge: "not mine".

**5B4 — is refusing disrespect?** [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 孟子/萬章下, section 4; translation mine]
- Wan Zhang: 敢問交際何心也？ "What heart is there in exchanging gifts?" — 恭也, "respect".
- 卻之卻之為不恭，何哉？ "Refusing it, refusing it, is counted disrespect; why?" — "When an honoured person bestows something, to ask 'was the way he got it right or not?' and only then accept, this is counted disrespect; therefore one does not refuse."
- 請無以辭卻之，以心卻之，曰：『其取諸民之不義也。』而以他辭無受，不可乎？ "Then let me not refuse it with words, but refuse it in my heart, saying [to myself] 'he took it from the people unjustly', and decline it on another pretext (他辭): is that not acceptable?" — 其交也以道，其接也以禮，斯孔子受之矣, "if the exchange is by the Way and the reception by ritual, then Confucius accepted."
- The chapter goes on to the case of a robber's gift (not to be accepted) and to Confucius's conduct in office; Mengzi does not endorse the pretext-refusal [verified].
- **Not free, said plainly:** refusing a superior's gift is "disrespect", and the disciple's way out is a refusal by pretext (他辭), the polite excuse that hides the real reason. Note that 辭 appears twice here in its sense "words": *以辭卻之*, "refuse with words", and *他辭*, "other words".

**5B6 — the refusal of a form of giving.** Duke Mu (繆公; Zhao Qi's commentary places this "in the time of Duke Mu of Lu", 魯繆公 [verified: zh.wikisource.org search, 孟子 (四部叢刊本)/卷第十, Zhao Qi's note]) repeatedly asked after Zisi and repeatedly sent him cooked meat. Zisi was displeased, and at last had the messenger taken out beyond the great gate, faced north, bowed his head to the ground twice and did not accept, saying: 今而後知君之犬馬畜汲 (the Wikisource text prints 汲; Zhu Xi's text has 伋, and Zhao Qi notes 伋，子思名也, "伋 is Zisi's personal name" [verified: 四書章句集註/孟子集注卷十; Zhao Qi as above]), "From now on I know that my lord keeps me as he keeps his dogs and horses." 蓋自是臺無饋也, "and from then on, it seems, the servants brought no more gifts." Mengzi faults the duke: "to delight in the worthy and be unable to raise him, or even to nourish him, can that be called delighting in the worthy?" [verified: 孟子/萬章下, section 6; translation mine].

Read to the end of the section: Wan Zhang asks how a ruler should nourish a gentleman. Mengzi: the first gift comes with the ruler's command and is received with two bows; after that the granary keeper keeps sending grain and the cook keeps sending meat, "not with the ruler's command". "Zisi held that the cauldron meat made him bow and bow again (僕僕爾亟拜); that is not the way to nourish a gentleman." The section ends with Yao nourishing Shun in the fields and then raising him [verified: same section]. Zhu Xi reads 蓋自是臺無饋也 as the duke's correction: 蓋繆公愧悟，自此不復令臺來致饋也, "Duke Mu was ashamed and saw his error, and from then on no longer had the servants bring gifts" [verified: 四書章句集註/孟子集注卷十].
- **Relation:** the text does not say the relation ended. What Zisi refuses is a form of giving that kept him bowing like a servant; Mengzi names the form that would have honoured him; Zhu Xi reads the stopped gifts as the duke's correction. Later histories that say Zisi then left Lu were not opened. The refusal is ritually perfect (bows, northward face) and aimed at the form, not the giver.

**5B5:** 辭尊居卑，辭富居貧, "decline honour and dwell in lowliness; decline wealth and dwell in poverty" [verified: 孟子/萬章下, section 5].

### 3.4 Zhuangzi, "Xiaoyao you" — Xu You: "not mine"

> 堯讓天下於許由，曰：「日月出矣，而爝火不息，其於光也，不亦難乎？… 夫子立而天下治，而我猶尸之，吾自視缺然，請致天下。」許由曰：「子治天下，天下旣已治也，而我猶代子，吾將爲名乎？名者，實之賓也，吾將爲賓乎？鷦鷯巢於深林，不過一枝；偃鼠飲河，不過滿腹。歸休乎君，予无所用天下爲！庖人雖不治庖，尸祝不越樽俎而代之矣。」
> "Yao yielded the world to Xu You, saying: 'When sun and moon have come out, to keep the torches burning, is that not hard on their light? … If you stood forth the world would be ordered, yet I still preside over it; I see my own lack; I ask to hand you the world.' Xu You said: 'You govern the world, and the world is already governed. If I took your place, would I be doing it for the name? The name is the guest of the reality: shall I be the guest? The wren nests in the deep forest and uses no more than one branch; the mole drinks at the river and takes no more than a bellyful. Go home and rest, my lord; I have no use for the world! Though the cook does not keep his kitchen, the impersonator of the dead and the invoker do not step over the wine-vessels and the offering-stands to take his place.'"
> [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 莊子/逍遙遊, raw text; translation mine. 歸休乎君 is rendered by translators in several ways ("go home", "give it up", "rest"); 休 is "rest, cease", and I give the literal sense.]

What this gives:
- **The decline is grounded in role, not in incapacity or a pretext.** The ritual specialist does not step over the vessels to do the cook's work. It is the clearest classical "not mine".
- **It ends with a release spoken to the offerer:** "go home and rest, my lord". The decliner sends the other back to his own place, with rest.
- **The offer itself is called 讓.** Yao "yields" the world. Offering and declining share the verb of yielding.
- **Relation:** the passage moves on at once to Jian Wu and Lian Shu [verified: same page]; the text does not say what happened between Yao and Xu You. That next episode ends with Yao himself: 往見四子藐姑射之山，汾水之陽，窅然喪其天下焉, "he went to see the four masters on Mount Miao Gushe, north of the Fen, and in a daze lost his world" [verified: same page]. The text does not name the four. Later legends (Xu You washing his ears) were not opened [from memory: not re-checked].

### 3.5 Shorter witnesses

- Lunyu 8.1: 泰伯，其可謂至德也已矣！三以天下讓，民無得而稱焉, "Taibo may be called of utmost virtue: three times he yielded the world, and the people found nothing to praise him for" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 論語/泰伯第八]. A threefold yielding that was real, and unseen.
- Liji "Zhongyong": 天下國家可均也，爵禄可辭也，白刃可蹈也，中庸不可能也, "the realm can be put in order, rank and salary can be declined, naked blades can be trodden; the Mean cannot be attained" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 禮記/中庸]. Declining office is listed among the hardest feats that are still possible.
- Lunyu 15.12 (numbered 十五之十一, 15.11, on the Wikisource page; the numbering of Book 15 differs between editions): 人無遠慮，必有近憂, "one without far-reaching deliberation will have near sorrows" [verified: zh.wikisource.org, 論語/衞靈公第十五]. This 遠慮 is the source of Japanese *enryo*; see archaeology/japanese/kotowaru-enryo.md.

## 4. Etymology and family

- **辭.** Unihan's definition is "words, speech, expression, phrase"; its Japanese kun reading is *yameru*, "to resign, quit" [verified: Unihan U+8FAD]. The Kangxi entry places "speech" first, the lawsuit sense second (Shuowen), and the refusal sense by identification with 辤 [verified]. Which sense is oldest I do not claim.
- **辤 = 辛 + 受.** The Shuowen's analysis, via Kangxi [verified]. Whether the graph's analysis reflects its history is a palaeographic question I did not open [from memory: not re-checked].
- **讓.** Shuowen "mutual reproach" first; "yield" later in the Kangxi ordering [verified]. The semantic path from rebuke to yielding is not explained in the sources opened.
- **Doctrinal family.** 辭 (decline, take leave) · 讓 (yield to another) · 辭讓 (the sprout of 禮) · 禮辭 / 固辭 (/ 終辭) (degrees of ritual decline) · 卻 (push back a gift) · 受 (accept) · 許 (consent) · 諾 / 唯 (the two yeses).
- **Pairing with acceptance.** Explicit and built into the script: 辤 is 不受; Mengzi's debates are framed as 受 / 不受 and 卻 / 受; the ritual decline closes with 許; and the Shijing line quoted in Kangxi s.v. 讓, 受爵不讓，至于已斯亡, "receiving rank without yielding, until one perishes", sets accepting-without-yielding as the fault [verified as quoted].

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

- The decline that is spoken as a leave-taking: words given as one goes.
- Passing on to another what was one's own due, so that the declining is a gift (讓).
- "Not mine": refusing an office because doing it would mean stepping over someone else's vessels (Xu You).
- Refusing a gift so that the relation does not become a purchase (Mengzi 2B3).

**Where the polite refusal is not free, said plainly:** the commonest Classical Chinese decline (禮辭, 固辭) is a stage of consent; the Liji requires it before answering an elder and forbids it before an elder's gift; Mengzi's disciple can only refuse a superior's gift by pretext (他辭); and the one real final refusal the commentary names, 終辭, is reached only as the third turn of the same script.

Near-misses:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| 辭 | dominant use is the ritual decline that expects a second request |
| 讓 | yielding to another; in modern Mandarin "let, allow" |
| 卻 | a gesture of pushing back a gift; "disrespect" toward a superior |
| 遠慮 | far deliberation; its "decline" sense is Japanese and later |

**Canon collision check of the region:** **renme** (canon/core/renme.md, 仁) and **daome** are the Confucian and Daoist donors in canon; neither names declining. **xeniame** names hospitality from the host's side. No canon word holds 辭 or 讓.

## 6. Convergence-mapping

Sinitic share of the cross-family picture:
1. **Decline lexicalised as speech or plea.** 辭 = words; the statements of a lawsuit; declining. Compare Latin *ex-/re-cusare* (< *causa*), Arabic *iʿtadhara ʿan*, Japanese *kotowaru*. Independent by descent; possibly typologically common [my inference].
2. **Decline written as not-accepting.** 辤 = 不受, with 受 inside it. Compare Hebrew *lā qabbəlītū* (b. Shabbat 88a) and Arabic *ʾabā* as fossilised "not want" (reported theory).
3. **The ritual refusal designed to be overridden.** 禮辭 / 固辭 (Yili; Liji). Compare Ruth Rabbah 2:16 (Hebrew), RB 58's door and *nolo episcopari* (Latin), *enryo* (Japanese, in contact with this vocabulary).
4. **The refusal that protects or releases.** Mengzi 2B3 (protects the relation from purchase); Xu You's 歸休乎君 (releases the offerer, "go home and rest"). Compare Elisha's "go in peace" (2 Kgs 5:19).
5. **The refusal of a form of giving, not of the giver.** Mengzi 5B6 (Zisi refuses gifts that kept him bowing; Mengzi names the right form; Zhu Xi reads the duke as corrected); Yili "Shi xiang jian li" (the great officer's 終辭 of the gift, with the visitor received and bowed out). The earlier draft paired 5B6 with Luke 14 as a ritually perfect refusal that ends a relation; read to its end, 5B6 does not say the relation ended.

Independence: native Sinitic. Japanese *jitai* and *enryo* are loans from it.

## 7. Connectable canon-words

- **renme** (仁; Core): the Confucian virtue the 辭讓 sprout sits beside in Mengzi 2A6.
- **daome** (道; Core): Xu You's refusal is a Daoist "no" to office.
- **xeniame** (Core, 8.15 per its file): host and guest; the Liji's 固辭 at the steps is hospitality's choreography.
- **walkekin** (Core): a bond that survives distance. Xu You's release ("go home and rest") does not say whether the bond survives.

## 8. Forge-targets identified — leads only

No lemma, no coinage, no score.

- **Lead C-D1 — 辭 cí.**
  - Carries: decline as speech and leave-taking; the plea; the variant written with "accept" inside it.
  - Weaknesses: [tsʰɹ̩] is unguessable from the spelling; toneless *ci* is shared by common Mandarin words such as 詞 "word" and 慈 "compassion" [from memory: not re-checked]; its dominant classical use is the ritual decline that is a stage of consent; 辤 = 不受 makes it the failure of a yes; *ci* sits near the *-tsi* of three Core headwords (§9).
- **Lead C-D2 — 讓 ràng.**
  - Carries: "not mine; yours": declining what was one's due in another's favour (應受而推); Taibo's threefold yielding; the same verb as the offer (Yao 讓 the world).
  - Weaknesses: modern Mandarin "let, allow" (and a passive marker [from memory]); the Shuowen's first sense is "reproach"; [ʐ] is hard for most learners; English *rang*.
- **Lead C-D3 — 終辭 zhōngcí, "the final refusal".**
  - Carries: a commentarial term (Zheng Xuan, verified §3.1) for the decline that is not a stage of consent; its textbook instance (Shi xiang jian li) refuses a gift while receiving the person.
  - Weaknesses: it is the third turn of the same ritual script, and is known as 終辭 only by its outcome (Jia: three declines followed by consent are merely 三辭); it is a commentator's term, not a word of the ritual text itself; its instance is a superior declining an inferior's gift; *zh* [ʈʂ] and the tone are lost in English.
- **Lead C-D4 — the pair 辭 / 受.**
  - Carries: the language's own accept/decline pair, with 受 shared between them.
  - Weaknesses: it is a pair about gifts, not tasks; it makes the decline a modification of the accept.
- **Lead C-D5 — 歸休 guīxiū (Zhuangzi).**
  - Carries: the decline that sends the offerer home with rest, "rest-as-a-whole-beat spoken to someone".
  - Weaknesses: a phrase in one text, not a term; translators differ; the relation afterwards is not narrated.
