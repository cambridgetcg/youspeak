# Excūsāre and recūsāre — refusing by pleading a cause

_The accept dig found the Rule of Benedict offering the novice, after long deliberation, the freedom "either to decline or to take up" (aut excusare aut suscipere), and found that the freedom ends at profession. This file opens the decline verb itself. Both Latin verbs of refusing are built on causa, a case at law: ex-cusare is to take oneself out of a charge, re-cusare to plead against one. Latin hears a refusal as a counter-plea. The law of guardianship shows what that means in practice: a citizen named as guardian could be excused only for recognised causes, within fifty days, and an excuse got by false statements did not free him. The Rule shows the monastic form: before profession, "if you cannot, depart free"; after it, a brother given impossible orders lays out "the causes of his impossibility" patiently, and if the superior persists, obeys out of love. The decline is a voice, not a veto. And Luke 14 shows the polite excuse at its most courteous ("I ask you, have me excused") and at its most costly: the host is angry, and none of those invited will taste his supper. The file closes on the false friend of the whole target, nolo episcopari, the refusal everyone understands to mean yes._

## 1. The concept in compact form

**excūsō, -āre** is glossed by Lewis & Short: "[causa; cf. accuso, from ad-causa; qs. to release from a charge, to free from blame; hence], to excuse a person or thing". Its senses: I. to excuse someone (*me tibi excuso*); II.A. to allege in excuse, to plead (*morbum*, *valetudinem*, *propinquitatem*); II.B. "to excuse, absolve one from any thing; to discharge, dispense with one", with *a coepta (tutela) excusari*, Dig. 27.1.11, and Vulg. Luc. 14.19 [verified: Lewis & Short s.v. excuso, via the Logeion API, anastrophe.uchicago.edu/logeion-api, which serves the Perseus L&S text].

**recūsō, -āre** is "[causa]. I. In gen., to make an objection against, in statement or reply; to decline, reject, refuse, be reluctant or unwilling to do a thing". II.2: "In jurid. lang., to protest against a complaint; to object, take exception, plead in defence", with Quintilian 3.10.1, *causa omnis, in quā pars altera agentis est, altera recusantis*, "every case, in which one side is the plaintiff's, the other the refuser's" [verified: L&S s.v. recuso, same source; translation of Quintilian mine]. **recūsātiō** in juristic language is "a plea in defence, counter-plea (opp. petitio)": Cic. Inv. 1.5.7, *aut petitionem et recusationem* [verified: L&S s.v. recusatio].

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, or consciously decline:**

1. **A Latin refusal is a plea in a case.** Both verbs are made from *causa*. *Recusatio* is the defendant's counter-plea, set against the plaintiff's *petitio*. In this language the decline answers a demand the way a defence answers a charge. That is a long way from the realm feature's "sovereign" refusal.
2. **The excuse from a public burden must be proved.** Guardianship is a *publicum munus*; one is excused *variis ex causis*, within fifty days, and "if anyone has obtained an excuse by false allegations, he is not freed from the burden" (Inst. 1.25, §3.1).
3. **The monastic decline is free before the vow and a voice after it.** RB 58: *si potes observare, ingredere; si vero non potes, liber discede*, "if you can keep it, enter; if you cannot, depart free". RB 68: under impossible orders, the brother should *suscipere* the command, lay out the causes of his impossibility *patienter et oportune*, "not with pride or resistance or contradiction", and if the superior persists, obey *ex caritate* (§3.2).
4. **The polite excuse can rupture.** Luke 14:18–24: *rogo te habe me excusatum*; the master is angry, and the parable closes "I tell you (plural), none of those men who were invited shall taste my supper" (§3.3).

**Sibling distinctions — four Latin ways of not doing what is asked:**
- **excusare** takes oneself out of the charge by showing a cause. The relation is not attacked; the burden is lifted, if the cause is good.
- **recusare** pleads against the demand. It is adversarial by its legal home.
- **abnuere / renuere** (listed by L&S as synonyms under recuso) refuse by a sign, a nod away [verified as listed; the entries themselves not opened].
- **liber discedere** declines by leaving before the bond is made.

## 3. Primary attestations, each read to its end

### 3.1 Justinian, Institutes 1.25 — the excuse from guardianship, which must be proved

> *Excusantur autem tutores vel curatores variis ex causis …* "Guardians and curators are excused for various causes."
> [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/justinian/institutes1.shtml, Lib. I Tit. XXV; translations here mine]

The title then lists the recognised causes: three living children at Rome (four in Italy, five in the provinces), "for it has been decided that both guardianship and curatorship are a public burden (*publicum munus*)"; sons lost in battle count, "for those who fell for the commonwealth are understood to live forever through glory"; administering the fisc; absence on public business; holding power (but *coeptam tutelam deserere non possunt*, "they cannot abandon a guardianship already begun"); poverty, "if anyone can show that he is unequal to the burden imposed"; illness; illiteracy (with the note that the illiterate can sometimes administer); enmity with the ward's father, if capital and unreconciled; age over seventy; certain teachers and doctors [verified].

And the limits:
- An excuse resting only on not having known the ward's father "is not to be admitted" [verified].
- Those who wish to be excused do not appeal; they must excuse themselves *intra dies quinquaginta continuos*, within fifty continuous days of learning of the appointment (more if they live beyond the hundredth milestone) [verified].
- "No one who has acted as guardian is compelled against his will (*invitus*) to become curator of the same person … he is not to be compelled to take up (*suscipere*) the curatorship against his will" (rescript of Severus and Antoninus) [verified].
- *Si quis autem falsis allegationibus excusationem tutelae meruit, non est liberatus onere tutelae*, "if anyone obtained an excuse from guardianship by false allegations, he is not freed from the burden" [verified].

**Relation:** the citizen's relation to the commonwealth is not broken by a good excuse; the burden passes to another. A bad excuse changes nothing: the burden stays. **Not free:** the decline is a petition with grounds that must be proved (*de quibusdam non probaverit*, "if he has not proved some of them", he may use others within the time), under a deadline. Who judges it is not named in this title. **Pairing with acceptance:** *excusare* stands against *suscipere* in the same title (*invitum eum curam suscipere non cogendum*), as in RB 58.

### 3.2 Regula Benedicti 58 and 68 — free to depart, then free to speak

**RB 58, read to its end** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/benedict.html, Caput 58]:
- The newcomer is not to be given easy entry: *non ei facilis tribuatur ingressus* ("test the spirits, whether they are of God"). Only if he keeps knocking and is seen "to bear patiently the injuries inflicted on him and the difficulty of entry" for four or five days is entry granted. **The community's first refusal is a test.** It is designed to be overcome by persistence.
- After two months the Rule is read to him: *Ecce lex sub qua militare vis; si potes observare, ingredere; si vero non potes, liber discede*, "See the law under which you wish to serve; if you can keep it, enter; if you cannot, depart free." It is read again after six months and after four more.
- Then, "having deliberated with himself", he is received, "knowing … that from that day he may not leave the monastery, nor shake his neck free from the yoke of the Rule, which after such long deliberation he was free either to decline or to take up (*aut excusare aut suscipere*)."
- The promise is written, laid on the altar, and sung (*Suscipe me, Domine*). His old clothes are kept "so that if ever he consents to the devil and leaves the monastery (God forbid), he may be stripped of the monastery's things and cast out (*proiciatur*)". His petition is kept.

**RB 68, whole** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/benedict.html, Caput 68]:

> *Si cui fratri aliqua forte gravia aut inpossibilia iniunguntur suscipiat quidem iubentis imperium cum omni mansuetudine et oboedientia. Quod si omnimo virium suarum mensuram viderit pondus oneris excedere, inpossibilitatis suæ causas ei qui sibi præest patienter et oportune suggerat, non superbiendo aut resistendo vel contradicendo. Quod si post suggestionem suam in sua sententia prioris imperium perduraverit, sciat iunior ita sibi expedire, et ex caritate, confidens de adiutorio Dei, oboediat.*
> "If heavy or impossible things are laid on a brother, let him receive the command of the one ordering with all gentleness and obedience. But if he sees that the weight of the burden altogether exceeds the measure of his strength, let him lay before his superior the causes of his impossibility, patiently and at a fitting time, not with pride or resistance or contradiction. And if after his representation the superior's order stands, let the junior know that it is good for him, and let him obey out of love, trusting in God's help." (translation mine)

What the two chapters give:
- **Before the bond:** a real, free decline (*liber discede*), offered as an equal option. The words *liber discede* are said once, at the first reading after two months; the Rule is then read twice more (after six months and after four), "so that he may know what he is entering", without the formula being repeated in the text. It leaves both sides free; nothing breaks, because nothing has yet been made. The relation is not kept; it is not formed.
- **After the bond:** the decline survives only as *causas suggerere*, "to suggest causes", and it does not bind the superior. The relation is kept, and it overrides the decline.
- **The community's own refusal at the door is a test.** That is the ritual refusal that is not free (§5).

### 3.3 Luke 14:15–24 — the polite excuse, and what it cost

Greek [verified: SBLGNT, raw.githubusercontent.com/morphgnt/sblgnt/master/63-Lk-morphgnt.txt, 14:16–24]; Latin [verified: vulgate.org/nt/gospel/luke_14.htm, 14:15–27]:

> 14:18 καὶ ἤρξαντο ἀπὸ μιᾶς πάντες παραιτεῖσθαι. ὁ πρῶτος εἶπεν αὐτῷ· Ἀγρὸν ἠγόρασα … ἐρωτῶ σε, ἔχε με παρῃτημένον.
> *et coeperunt simul omnes excusare primus dixit ei villam emi … rogo te habe me excusatum*
> "And they all alike began to excuse themselves. The first said to him: I have bought a field … I ask you, have me excused." (translation mine)

The second has bought five yoke of oxen, with the same formula (14:19). The third: "I have married a wife, and therefore I cannot come" (14:20). Read to the end of the parable:
- 14:21: ὀργισθεὶς ὁ οἰκοδεσπότης, *iratus pater familias*: the householder, angry, sends for the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame.
- 14:23: ἀνάγκασον εἰσελθεῖν, *conpelle intrare*, "compel them to come in, that my house may be filled".
- 14:24: "none of those men who were invited shall taste my supper" [verified, Greek and Latin].

**Relation:** broken by the host, permanently. The excuses are courteous, specific and plausible; the parable treats them as refusal of the host himself. **Pairing:** the Vulgate's *excusare* translates παραιτεῖσθαι, and L&S cites Vulg. Luc. 14:19 for the sense "to excuse, absolve one from" [verified]. This is contact: Latin here renders Greek. **A caution:** 14:23 *conpelle intrare* was later used to justify coercion in religion: Augustine, writing to the Donatist Vincentius in 408, answers the view that no one should be compelled to righteousness with "you read that the householder said to his servants, Whomsoever you shall find, compel them to come in" [verified: Augustine, Ep. 93.2.5, New Advent English, newadvent.org/fathers/1102093.htm; the Latin not opened]. A decline-word that cites this parable inherits a text in which the answer to a courteous refusal is exclusion of the refusers and compulsion of others.

**LSJ s.v. παραιτέομαι** [verified: LSJ via the Logeion API]: "beg of or from another, ask as a favour of him" (Pl. Ap. 27b, etc.); "intercede with, appeal to a person"; "beg one's pardon"; II.1 "avert by entreaty, deprecate", including τὸ ἀποθανεῖν (Act.Ap. 25.11, Paul: "I do not beg off from dying"); II.2 "decline, deprecate, χάριν Pi. N. 10.30"; "b. demand exemption from" farming or office (PLond. 3.1231.3, ii A.D.; POxy. 1252v28, iii A.D.); II.3 "ask him to excuse one, decline his invitation, Plb. 5.27.3 … abs., Ev.Luc. 14.18:—Pass., ἔχε με παρῃτημένον"; also παραιτησάμενος Ἔφορον, "Lat. *pace Ephori*", "begging Ephorus's pardon", i.e. disagreeing with him courteously; II.4 "divorce" a wife, "dismiss" a servant; III. "intercede for, beg off, esp. from punishment" (Hdt. 3.119).

One Greek verb therefore covers asking for something, interceding for someone, declining a favour, claiming exemption from a public burden (the papyri), courteous disagreement, and divorce. It runs from the most bond-keeping act to the most bond-ending one. The papyrus sense "demand exemption from office" is the Greek face of the Latin *excusatio a muneribus*.

### 3.4 *Nolo episcopari* — the false friend

Wiktionary's English entry: from Latin "I do not want to be bishop"; "It was formerly maintained that those nominated for the post of bishop politely declined the offer twice with these words". Sense: "Expressing a facetiously modest refusal of something which is actually desired" [verified: en.wiktionary.org/wiki/nolo_episcopari]. Its quotations include Fielding's *Tom Jones*, which applies the phrase to a woman's first answer to a suitor, "the answer which was first made some thousands of years ago, and which hath been handed down by tradition from mother to daughter ever since" [verified as quoted there]. They also include Chesterton's *Orthodoxy*, which uses the phrase the other way: "Carlyle's hero may say, 'I will be king'; but the Christian saint must say '*Nolo episcopari*' … we must … go hunting … until we find the one man who feels himself unfit to wear it" [verified as quoted there]. For Chesterton the formula is a sincere refusal from felt unfitness, and the refusal is what qualifies the refuser.

- The historical claim about bishops is reported as "formerly maintained"; I found no primary evidence of such a formula and do not assert it [unverified].
- The English sense Wiktionary gives is the clearest statement in the dig of **the polite refusal that is not free**: a no that every party understands as a yes. Wiktionary's own quotations show the phrase carrying both readings, the facetious one and the sincere one.
- Fielding's use shows where the facetious reading leads: a woman's refusal is read as ritual, to be overridden. A decline-word must be built so that it cannot be read this way. That is the most important negative finding of the Latin dig. Chesterton's use shows the second danger: a sincere refusal that is taken as proof of fitness, and so invites the office it declines.

### 3.5 Shorter witnesses (as quoted in Lewis & Short)

- *terra numquam recusat imperium*, "the earth never refuses a command" (Cic. Sen. 15.51): refusal as what the obedient never does [verified as quoted in L&S s.v. recuso].
- *non recuso, non abnuo* (Cic. Mil. 36.100) [verified as quoted].
- *qui quod ab altero postularent, in se recusarent*, "who refused for themselves what they demanded from another" (Caes. B. C. 1.32.5) [verified as quoted].
- *turpis enim excusatio est si quis contra rem publicam se amici causa fecisse fateatur*, "for it is a shameful excuse if someone admits he acted against the commonwealth for a friend's sake" (Cic. Lael. 12.40): friendship is not a cause [verified as quoted in L&S s.v. excusatio].

## 4. Etymology and family

- **excūsō, recūsō < causa** (L&S, both entries) [verified]. *accūsō* "accuse" is the same formation with *ad-* (L&S, cited in the excuso entry) [verified as cited]. Accusing, excusing and refusing are three prepositions on one legal noun.
- **causa** itself: etymology uncertain [from memory: not re-checked; de Vaan not opened].
- **παραιτέομαι** < παρά + αἰτέομαι, "ask" [from memory: the compound analysis is standard; not re-checked]. The Greek decline is built on asking, the Latin on pleading.
- **English descendants** (not donor evidence, only the English ear): *excuse* (apology; pretext); *recuse*, from Old French *recuser* and Latin *recūsāre* (re- + *causa*) [verified: en.wiktionary.org/wiki/recuse]; *recusant*, "someone refusing to attend Church of England services, between the 16th and early 19th centuries", from *recūsāns* [verified: en.wiktionary.org/wiki/recusant] (that recusants were fined is from memory, not re-checked). English *refuse* comes through Old French *refuser* from Vulgar Latin *\*refūsāre*, which Wiktionary derives either from a blend of *refūtāre* and *recūsāre* or from *refūsus* (*refundere*) [verified: en.wiktionary.org/wiki/refuse, English, Etymology 2]. So *recūsāre* may be one parent of *refuse*; the earlier statement that it is not was wrong.
- **Doctrinal family.** *excusatio* (exemption pleaded) · *recusatio* (counter-plea) · *petitio* (the demand) · *munus* (the burden) · *suscipere* (taking up; archaeology/latin/suscipere.md) · *liber discedere* (leaving before the bond).

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

What the Latin record shows, and English says only at length:
- Declining before a bond is formed, and leaving free, with nothing owed (RB 58 before profession).
- Declining inside a bond as a representation, "these are the causes of my impossibility", that does not claim to bind the other (RB 68).
- The difference between an excuse that lifts a burden (a cause) and an excuse that offends (a pretext, Luke 14).

**Where a polite refusal is in fact not free, said plainly:**
- *Nolo episcopari*: a refusal understood as acceptance.
- RB 58's door: the community refuses entry so that the newcomer will persist.
- Inst. 1.25: the citizen cannot simply say no; he must prove a cause, in time.
- RB 68: after the vow, the decline is only a voice.
- Luke 14: the courteous excuse costs the invitation for good.

Near-misses, each failing:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| *excusare* | a decline that must give a cause and can be judged; in English it reads as apology or pretext |
| *recusare* | a counter-plea in a case: adversarial by home |
| *liber discede* | free, but a leaving: the relation is not kept, it is not formed |
| *causas suggerere* (RB 68) | keeps the relation, but the decline does not hold |
| παραιτέομαι | spans intercession and divorce; its best-known decline (Luke 14) ruptures |

**Canon collision check of the region:** no canon word holds the decline. The near-neighbours are named in archaeology/hebrew/meun-refusal.md §5 (sinbyeongme, walkekin, warame). On the Latin side, canon/core/xeniame.md (hospitality to the stranger) is the host's side of Luke 14's banquet; it does not name the guest's decline.

## 6. Convergence-mapping

Patterns witnessed here, with the other families' witnesses named for orientation (each is argued in its own file):

1. **Decline lexicalised as pleading or giving reasons.** Latin *ex-/re-cusare* (< *causa*). Also Arabic *iʿtadhara ʿan* (archaeology/arabic/iba-itidhar.md), Classical Chinese 辭 (also "pleading statement", archaeology/chinese/ci-rang-declining.md), Japanese *kotowaru* (< "divide the matter" → explain → decline, archaeology/japanese/kotowaru-enryo.md). Four families, independent by descent. **Caution:** "excuse oneself" for "decline" may be cross-linguistically common [my inference; no typological source checked], so this is weak evidence of anything special. And it cuts against the target: these languages build the no as an account owed.
2. **Decline offered as an equal option.** RB 58 (*aut excusare aut suscipere*; *si potes … si non potes*). Hebrew Ruth 4:4; the min of b. Shabbat 88a.
3. **The ritual refusal designed to be overridden.** RB 58's door; *nolo episcopari*. Also Hebrew (Ruth Rabbah 2:16), Chinese (Yili 禮辭), Japanese (enryo).
4. **The polite refusal that ruptures.** Luke 14:18–24.

Independence: Latin and Greek are one family; Luke's Latin is a translation of its Greek. The Roman-law excusatio is native Roman and independent of the Semitic material; the RB is in contact with biblical tradition.

- **Lead L-D1 — excus- (excūsāre).**
  - Carries: the decline named as an equal alternative to taking up (RB 58); release from a burden without breach (Inst. 1.25, when the cause is good).
  - Weaknesses: the decline must give a cause and can be refused (Inst. 1.25); English *excuse* is apology or pretext; its best-known use (Luke 14) ruptured.
- **Lead L-D2 — recus- (recūsāre).**
  - Carries: the plain verb of refusing.
  - Weaknesses: a counter-plea against a petitio, adversarial by its legal home; English *recuse*, *recusant*.
- **Lead L-D3 — *liber discede*.**
  - Carries: the decline that leaves both sides free, said by the one offering, before any bond.
  - Weaknesses: a phrase; it names leaving, not a no that keeps a relation; the freedom ends at the vow.
- **Lead L-D4 — Greek paraite- (παραιτέομαι).**
  - Carries: one verb for asking for, interceding, declining, claiming exemption and courteous disagreement (*pace Ephori*).
  - Weaknesses: it also means "divorce"; Luke 14 is its paradigm decline and it ruptured; same family as Latin; Greek is the canon's most-mined tongue; *para-* is a busy English prefix.
