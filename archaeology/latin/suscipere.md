# Suscipere — "to take hold of in order to support"

_The forge target's sketched donor pair names `latin/suscipere` beside the Hebrew. This dig opens that verb and the Latin vocabulary of pledged uptake around it. Lewis & Short draws a line that the target's realm feature ("binding acceptance") straddles: **suscipio** is used "esp. when done voluntarily and as a favor; **recipio**, when done as a duty or under an obligation". A forge that wants the binding needs to know that Latin puts the binding mostly in recipere, spondere and stipulatio, and the free taking-up in suscipere. The dig also finds a Latin answer to the min of b. Shabbat 88a: the Rule of Benedict has the Rule read to the novice with the words "if you can keep it, enter; if you cannot, depart free" (si potes observare, ingredere; si vero non potes, liber discede), makes him deliberate at length, free "either to decline or to take up" (aut excusare aut suscipere), and only then binds him by a written promise and has him sing "Suscipe me". Read to its end, the chapter also shows the cost: after profession, a monk who leaves is stripped and cast out, and his written promise is kept._

The Roman-law material (Gaius, the Digest, Cicero) is a closed corpus with no living custodians. The liturgical material is living. ## 1. The concept in compact form

**suscipiō, -ere, -cēpī, -ceptum** is *sus-* (Lewis & Short: "a contraction of subs, for sub") + *capiō*, "take". L&S glosses the compound "qs. to take hold of in order to support, i.e. to take or catch up, to take upon one" [verified: L&S s.v. suscipio, perseus.tufts.edu, Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=suscipio]. Its senses run in a clear arc:

1. **Hold up from below.** Of buildings: *lapideas moles, quibus porticus suscipimus*, "the stone masses with which we hold up colonnades" (Sen. Ep. 90.26, as L&S quotes it).
2. **Take upon oneself, undertake.** "Esp. when done voluntarily and as a favor; recipio, when done as a duty or under an obligation." Examples: *inimicitias aut laborem aut sumptus suscipere nolunt*, "they are unwilling to take on enmities or toil or expense" (Cic. Off. 1.9.28); a war, a cause, a vow (*votum*, Plaut. Am.; Liv. 27.45.8).
3. **Undergo, accept** feelings and experiences: *dolorem* (Cic. Tusc. 1.46.111), *odium*, *invidiam*.
4. **Catch, take up, receive.**
   - (a) **"To take up a new-born child from the ground; hence, to acknowledge, recognize, bring up as one's own"** (Cic. Tusc. 3.1.2; Ter. And.).
   - (b) To beget or bear children (*filiam ex uxore*).
   - (c) To receive someone as a citizen, a pupil or a candidate: *Cato cum esset Tusculi natus, in populi Romani civitatem susceptus est* (Cic. Leg. 2.2.5); *suscipe me totum* (Vatinius in Cic. Fam. 5.9.1).
5. **Take up a speech, answer**: *suscipit Stolo: Tu, inquit, invides* … (Varro RR 1.2.24); Verg. A. 6.723.

[All quotations in this list: verified as quoted in L&S s.v. suscipio. Of the primary texts I opened only Cic. Tusc. 3.1.2 (§3).]

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, or consciously decline:**

1. **Taking up is from below.** *Sub-* puts the taker underneath what is taken up. Bearing, supporting and acknowledging are one gesture. (Chinese has a structurally similar gloss for 承 chéng, 下載上也, "the lower carries the upper"; see archaeology/hebrew/uptake-convergence-notes.md. That is a parallel, not a relation.)
2. **The taking-up is free.** L&S's distinction (suscipio voluntary, recipio obligated) is drawn by Cicero himself in one sentence (Verr. 2.2.1, §3). If the forge wants the *binding* of a commission entrusted by another, Latin's word for that is **recipere**. Suscipere is the yes that goes beyond what was owed.
3. **Uptake in dialogue.** Suscipere also means "to take up the speech, answer" (Varro, Vergil). The verb itself spans "take on a task" and "reply to a speaker".
4. **The binding answer belongs to another verb family.** The Roman *verborum obligatio* binds by question and answer, *Spondes? Spondeo* (Gaius 3.92), and that family is spondere/promittere/stipulari, not suscipere. It is the main verbal contract, not the only one: Gaius goes on to verbal obligations made by one speaker without a question (the *dotis dictio*, 3.95a, and a freedman's sworn promise to his patron, 3.96) [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/gaius3.html].

**Sibling distinctions — five verbs, five registers:**

- **suscipere**: free taking-up from below; support; acknowledgement; answering in dialogue.
- **recipere**: taking on what was entrusted (*fidei commissum*); it becomes a guarantee in *in me recipio*.
- **spondere**: the solemn binding answer, originally ritual (L&S: "akin with σπένδω, to pour out [a libation]; cf. σπονδαί, league"); citizens only.
- **promittere**: sending one's word forward; also "to accept an invitation", the dinner sense.
- **oboedire**: hearing-toward; the only one of the five built on *audire*.

The forge target's phrase "hearing that completes itself in pledged uptake" needs pieces of three of them: *oboedire* (hearing), *suscipere/recipere* (uptake), *spondere* (pledge). No single Latin verb carries all three.

## 3. Primary attestations and scholarly references

**Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 3.1.2 — the newborn "taken up"** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/tusc3.shtml]:

> *Nunc autem, simul atque editi in lucem et suscepti sumus, in omni continuo pravitate et in summa opinionum perversitate versamur, ut paene cum lacte nutricis errorem suxisse videamur.*
> "But as it is, as soon as we are brought forth into the light and taken up, we find ourselves at once in every kind of depravity and the utmost perversity of opinions, so that we seem almost to have sucked in error with our nurse's milk." (translation mine)

*Complication.* Cicero says only *editi in lucem et suscepti*: brought into the light and taken up. His next sentence goes on: *Cum vero parentibus redditi, dein magistris traditi sumus* ("but when we have been handed back to our parents, then given over to teachers") [verified: same page]. In this sequence the parents come after the taking-up and the nurse, so (my reading) *suscepti* here need not name a father's act at all. The picture in L&S's gloss, a father ceremonially lifting the newborn "from the ground", has been argued to be a modern construction. Brent D. Shaw, "Raising and Killing Children: Two Roman Myths", *Mnemosyne* 54 (2001) 31–77, argues against both the lifting ritual and the father's legal power to kill [bibliographic details and the article's thesis: from search-result summaries only; the article was not opened, and I could not check how it treats *suscipere* as distinct from *tollere*]. A forge should not build on "the father lifting the child from the earth" as attested ritual.

**Cicero, In Verrem 2.2.1 — recepi vs. suscepi in one sentence** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/verres.2.2.shtml, §1]:

> *Multa mihi necessario, iudices, praetermittenda sunt, ut possim aliquo modo aliquando de his rebus quae meae fidei commissae sunt dicere. **Recepi** enim causam Siciliae: ea me ad hoc negotium provincia attraxit. Ego tamen hoc onere **suscepto** et **recepta** causa Siciliensi amplexus animo sum aliquanto amplius. **Suscepi** enim causam totius ordinis, **suscepi** causam rei publicae …*
> "… so that I may speak of the matters entrusted to my good faith. For I *accepted* (recepi) the cause of Sicily: it was the province that drew me into this business. Yet having taken up this burden and accepted the Sicilian cause, I embraced in my mind something considerably larger. For I *took up* (suscepi) the cause of the whole order, I took up the cause of the commonwealth …" (translation mine)

*Recepi* is the commission entrusted to his *fides* by the Sicilians. *Suscepi* is what he took on beyond it, of his own accord. This is L&S's distinction in Cicero's own mouth, and the locus that matters most for a forge choosing between them.

**Gaius, Institutes 3.92–94 — the verbal obligation** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/gaius3.html; the Greek is given there in transliteration, partly garbled by the page's encoding, and I restore it with standard accents]:

> *92. Verbis obligatio fit ex interrogatione et responsione, uelut DARI SPONDES? SPONDEO, DABIS? DABO, PROMITTIS? PROMITTO, FIDEPROMITTIS? FIDEPROMITTO, FIDEIVBES? FIDEIVBEO, FACIES? FACIAM.*
> "An obligation is created by words through question and answer, for example: 'Do you pledge that it will be given? I pledge.' 'Will you give? I will give.' 'Do you promise? I promise.' 'Do you promise on your faith? I so promise.' 'Do you order it on your faith [as guarantor]? I so order.' 'Will you do it? I will do it.'"

> *93. Sed haec quidem uerborum obligatio DARI SPONDES? SPONDEO propria ciuium Romanorum est; ceterae uero iuris gentium sunt … et quamuis ad Graecam uocem expressae fuerint, uelut hoc modo "δώσεις; δώσω, ὁμολογεῖς; ὁμολογῶ, πίστει κελεύεις; πίστει κελεύω, ποιήσεις; ποιήσω", etiam hae tamen inter ciues Romanos ualent, si modo Graeci sermonis intellectum habeant … at illa uerborum obligatio DARI SPONDES? SPONDEO adeo propria ciuium Romanorum est, ut ne quidem in Graecum sermonem per interpretationem proprie transferri possit, quamuis dicatur a Graeca uoce figurata esse.*
> "But this verbal obligation, 'Do you pledge? I pledge', is proper to Roman citizens; the others belong to the law of nations … and even if expressed in Greek — 'will you give? I will give; **do you agree? I agree (homologeîs? homologô)**; do you order on faith? I order on faith; will you do it? I will do it' — these too are valid between Roman citizens, provided they understand Greek … But the obligation 'Do you pledge? I pledge' is so proper to Roman citizens that it cannot even be properly translated into Greek, although it is said to be formed from a Greek word."

> *94.* The one case where a foreigner can be bound by *spondeo* is when the emperor asks a foreign prince *PACEM FVTVRAM SPONDES?* Gaius calls this "too subtly said", because a breach is handled not by an action on the stipulation but by the law of war.

What this gives a forge:
- **The binding sits in the answer.** The one who will be bound speaks second, repeating the verb of the question in the first person. Structurally this is an offer → accept exchange.
- **Validity needs the parties to understand the language used** (*si modo Graeci sermonis intellectum habeant*, and conversely for Latin among foreigners). This is a condition on understanding the words, narrower than Sforno's "that they would know what they were accepting" (archaeology/hebrew/naaseh-venishma.md §3.2). The resemblance is loose; it is not the same condition.
- **The answer is not the only binding form.** In 3.95a–96 Gaius records verbal obligations created by one speaker without a question (*uno loquente et sine interrogatione*): the *dotis dictio* and a freedman's sworn promise to his patron.
- **The most binding word was the most exclusive.** *Spondeo* was reserved to citizens and untranslatable. A coinage that leans on *spond-* inherits a word whose force came from excluding.
- The Greek equivalent list puts **ὁμολογέω** (homologéō, "agree, concede; promise") in the stipulation slot. That is contact evidence linking the Greek and Latin witnesses.

L&S adds the Digest's own definitions [verified as quoted in L&S s.v. stipulatio; the Digest itself not opened]:
- *stipulatio est verborum conceptio, quibus is qui interrogatur, daturum facturumve se, quod interrogatus est, respondet* (Dig. 45.1.5, as L&S lays out the citation);
- ***stipulatio non potest confici, nisi utroque loquente***, "a stipulation cannot be made unless both speak" (Dig. 45.1.1).

**A pledge disowned: the Caudine Forks.** L&S s.v. sponsio and spondeo quote Livy 9: *non foedere pax Caudina sed per sponsionem facta est* (9.5.2); *ea demum sponsio esset, quam populi iussu spopondissemus* (9.9.13); *tunc sponsio et pax repudietur* (9.11.5) [verified as quoted in L&S; Livy not opened]. The consuls' sponsio was repudiated by the Roman people, and in Livy's narrative the sponsors were handed over to the enemy [narrative detail from memory: not re-checked]. An uptake given by an agent can be disowned by the principal. For a wire protocol in which a citizen accepts on behalf of another, this is the cautionary case.

**Regula Benedicti 58 — deliberation, then decline-or-take-up, then promise, then "Suscipe me"** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/benedict.html, ch. 58]:

> *… sciens et lege regulae constitutum quod ei ex illa die non liceat egredi monasterio, nec collum excutere de sub iugo regulae quem sub tam morosam deliberationem licuit aut **excusare** aut **suscipere**. Suscipiendus autem in oratorio coram omnibus **promittat** de stabilitate sua et conversatione morum suorum et **oboedientia**, coram Deo et sanctis eius, ut si aliquando aliter fecerit, ab eo se damnandum sciat quem inridit. De qua **promissione** sua faciat **petitionem** … Quam petitionem manu sua scribat … et manu sua eam super altare ponat. Quam dum inposuerit, incipiat ipse novicius mox hunc versum: **Suscipe me, Domine, secundum eloquium tuum et vivam, et ne confundas me ab expectatione mea.** Quem versum omnis congregatio tertio respondeat, adiungentes: Gloria Patri.*
> "… knowing that by the law of the Rule it is established that from that day he may not leave the monastery, nor shake his neck free from under the yoke of the Rule, which after such long deliberation he was free either to **decline** or to **take up**. The one who is to be received (*suscipiendus*) shall **promise** in the oratory before all, before God and his saints, stability, conversion of life and **obedience** … Of this promise he shall make a written petition … write it with his own hand … and with his own hand place it on the altar. When he has laid it there, the novice himself shall at once begin this verse: '**Take me up, Lord, according to your word, and I shall live; and do not let me be put to shame in my hope.**' The whole community shall answer this verse three times, adding Gloria Patri." (translation mine)

Earlier in the same chapter, after two months, the Rule is read to the newcomer and he is told: *Ecce lex sub qua militare vis; si potes observare, ingredere; si vero non potes, liber discede*, "See the law under which you wish to serve: if you can keep it, enter; if you cannot, depart free". It is read to him again after six months and after four more [verified: same page]. This is almost the min's sentence in b. Shabbat 88a ("if you were able, you would have accepted; if not, not").

The chapter does not end with the verse. The novice then gives away his property, "knowing that from that day he will not have power even over his own body". He is stripped of his own clothes and dressed in the monastery's. His old clothes are stored "so that if he should ever consent to the devil and leave the monastery (God forbid), he may be stripped of the monastery's things and cast out (*proiciatur*)". His petition is not given back but kept in the monastery [verified: same page]. The freedom to decline is real before profession and ends at it. After profession, leaving is rupture.

This one rite holds every element the forge target names, and the Hebrew sugya's critic besides:
- deliberation before acceptance (the min's demand), with the Rule read aloud three times;
- the freedom to decline (*excusare*), named as an equal option, until profession;
- the voluntary taking-up of a yoke (*suscipere iugum*; compare *qabbālat ʿōl*, m. Berakhot 2:2);
- a binding promise of obedience, spoken and also written and laid on the altar;
- a prayer in which the one who accepts asks to be accepted (*Suscipe me*);
- the community's threefold answer;
- a sanction: the written promise is kept, and a professed monk who leaves is cast out.

Acceptance here runs both ways: the novice takes up the yoke and asks to be taken up. (The "yoke" image shared with *ʿōl malkhūt šāmayim* is not independent. Christian Latin received it through the Gospels, Mt 11:29–30, from Second Temple Jewish usage [from memory: not re-checked].)

**Vulgate Psalm 118(119)** [verified: vulgate.org/ot/psalms_118.htm, which prints two Latin versions in parallel]:
- v. 116, *suscipe me secundum eloquium tuum et vivam et non confundas me ab expectatione mea* (the version RB 58 uses; RB reads *ne confundas* for *non confundas*); the parallel version reads *confirma me secundum verbum tuum …*. That the *suscipe me* text is the Gallican Psalter and the other the "iuxta Hebraeos" version is [from memory: not re-checked].
- v. 114, *adiutor meus et susceptor meus es tu*: "my helper and my *susceptor*", one who takes me up, a protector.

**The other "Suscipe" prayers.**
- Ignatius of Loyola, *Spiritual Exercises* §234 (the "Contemplatio ad amorem"): *Suscipe, Domine, universam meam libertatem …*, "Take, Lord, receive all my liberty" [verified: catholic-resources.org/Ignatian-Prayers.htm (Felix Just, S.J.), which gives the Latin and attributes it to SpEx #234 without naming the Latin version]. The Spanish autograph reads *Tomad, Señor, y recibid* … [from memory: not re-checked]. One search-result source said the prayer draws on monastic profession and Ps 118 [search snippet only; not opened].
- The offertory prayer of the pre-1970 Roman Missal, *Suscipe, sancte Pater, omnipotens aeterne Deus, hanc immaculatam hostiam* [from memory: not re-checked].
- *Complication for the forge.* In both prayers the imperative *suscipe* is addressed to God. The speaker is the **offerer** asking to be accepted, which is the side canon/worship-action/qorvance.md already names. Only in RB 58 is a human the one who *suscipit*, and even there he also prays *suscipe me*. The liturgical *Suscipe* mostly names uptake **asked for**, not uptake **given**.

**Oboedire — hearing-toward.**
- Vulgate Ex 24:7 renders *nišmāʿ* as *erimus oboedientes* [verified: vulgate.org/ot/exodus_24.htm].
- RB 58 places *oboedientia* in the novice's promise [verified].
- L&S s.v. oboedio: "ob-audio. I. In gen. (very rare), to give ear, hearken … (Nep. Dat. 5.4) — II. … to obey, yield obedience to"; *parere et oboedire praecepto* (Cic. Tusc. 5.12.36); *obtemperare et oboedire magistratibus* (Cic. Leg. 3.2.5); *dicto audiens atque oboediens* (Liv. 38.7) [verified as quoted in L&S].
- Descendants and loan-renderings: French *obéir* and English *obey* come from it [from memory: not re-checked]. German ***gehorsam*** arose as a rendering of Latin *oboediens*. DWDS (Pfeifer): "Das Adjektiv entsteht als Wiedergabe des den christlichen Gehorsamkeitsbegriff … ausdrückenden lat. *oboediēns* … Das Germ. knüpft an das dem lat. Wort zugrundeliegende Simplex lat. *audīre* ‘hören’ an" [verified: dwds.de/wb/etymwb/gehorsam]. The Germanic "hear-to-obey" word in its Christian sense is therefore a calque. It is contact, not an independent witness.

**Greek side (contact witnesses)** [verified: LSJ via Perseus, Perseus:text:1999.04.0057]:

## 4. Etymology and family

- **suscipiō** < *subs-* + *capiō* (L&S [verified]). *capiō* "take, seize" is generally referred to PIE *kh₂p- "grasp" [from memory: not re-checked]. The compound pattern *sub-* + verb of taking also gives *suscipere*'s sense-arc of support → undertake → receive.
- **spondeō**: L&S "akin with σπένδω, to pour out, = libare; cf. σπονδαί, league" [verified]. Gaius reports that the word was "said to be formed from a Greek word" [verified]. The pledge is, at its root, a libation, and the binding answer once had a sacrificial act under it.
- **prōmittō** < *prō* + *mittō*: "to let go forward" (L&S I. Lit.) [verified]. The promise is a word sent ahead of oneself.
- **recipiō** < *re-* + *capiō* [verified, L&S].
- **oboediō** < *ob* + *audiō* (L&S) [verified]. The irregular *oe* (for expected *au*) is discussed in the etymological literature [from memory: not re-checked; de Vaan not opened].
- **stipulātiō** < *stipulor*. Its etymology is disputed (ancient connection with *stipula*, "straw", or with *stips*, "coin") [from memory: not re-checked].

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

- Accepting a task you were not obliged to take, so that the acceptance itself is the gift (Cicero's *suscepi* beyond *recepi*).
- The "I will" that is binding only because it was asked for. In a stipulation the same words volunteered unasked would be a mere statement (*nisi utroque loquente*). Gaius 3.96 records exceptions, sworn one-speaker promises that also bind.
- Being free to decline, having deliberated, and taking it up anyway (RB 58), knowing that the freedom ends with the yes.

Near-misses, each failing:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| English *undertake* | the closest English verb; has lost the "from below / support" image and the dialogue sense; bureaucratic; homonym with the funeral trade |
| English *susceptible* | a false friend: from the same verb but means "liable to be affected", passive and the opposite of agency |
| *sponsor* | names the guarantor of another, not the one who takes something on |
| Latin *oboedire* alone | carries the hearing but loses the freedom; it is obedience, with RB 58's yoke |
| Latin *recipere* alone | carries the binding but not the free gift, and has no hearing |

- **Lead L4 — the pair recepi/suscepi itself.**
  - What it suggests: the target may be two performatives, not one: accepting the commission given (recipere), and taking up more than was asked (suscipere).

### 1. Correction: "not binding per L&S"

**Old claim.** §9 (line 242) gives *suscipere*'s weakness as "not binding per L&S". The lede (line 16: "Latin puts the binding mostly in recipere, spondere and stipulatio, and the free taking-up in suscipere") and Lead L1's first weakness (line 221) lean the same way. §1 property 2 (line 49) states L&S's distinction correctly.

**What L&S says** [verified: L&S as served by the Logeion API, anastrophe.uchicago.edu/logeion-api, opened 2026-11-11; the Perseus page refused the request (HTTP 403)]:
- s.v. suscipio I.B.2: "To take upon one, undertake, assume, begin, incur, enter upon (esp. when done voluntarily and as a favor; recipio, when done as a duty or under an obligation). α Of actions, obligations, etc. (class. and freq.)". Its examples include *aes alienum amicorum* (Cic. Off. 2, 16, 56) and *votum* (Plaut. Am.; Liv. 27, 45, 8).
- s.v. recipio II.B.2.a: "To take upon one's self, undertake, accept the performance of a task consigned or intrusted to one (whereas suscipio denotes, in gen., the voluntary undertaking of any action; cf.: spondeo, stipulor, polliceor)".

**Corrected claim.** L&S separates two grounds for an undertaking: the taker's own choice (*suscipio*) and a duty or a trust given by another (*recipio*). It marks the line with "esp.", and it lists obligations among the things one takes up. It does not say that what is freely taken up fails to bind. The primary texts read for this erratum do not say so either:
- Cicero, *de Oratore* 2.101 (L&S s.v. recipio cites it as 2, 24, 101, and prints it without *quidem* or the commas): *in quo est illa quidem magna offensio vel neglegentiae, susceptis rebus, vel perfidiae, receptis*, "in which there is indeed that great offence: of negligence, in matters taken up, or of bad faith, in matters accepted on trust" (translation mine) [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/oratore2.shtml, §101, opened 2026-11-11]. Failing either is a fault, but not the same fault: negligence for what was taken up, and a breach of faith (*perfidia*) only for what was accepted on trust.
- RB 58 (quoted in §3): the yoke the novice "was free either to decline or to take up" is the yoke he may not afterwards shake off, and the next sentence has him promise [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/benedict.html, Caput 58, opened 2026-11-11].
- Cicero, Verr. 2.2.1 (quoted in §3): Cicero speaks of *hoc onere suscepto*, "this burden taken up". He does not say whether what he took up binds him. In that sentence, *hoc onere suscepto et recepta causa Siciliensi* uses *suscipere* of "this burden" and *recipere* of the Sicilian cause. By my reading, "this burden" points back to *hoc negotium*, the prosecution Sicily drew him into, so here both verbs touch the commissioned case [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/verres.2.2.shtml, §1, opened 2026-11-11; the reading is mine]. The forge's assessor wrote that *suscepi causam* "commits him" to the prosecution. In the passage, *suscepi causam* is said of the causes Cicero embraced beyond the Sicilian one (*amplexus animo sum aliquanto amplius. Suscepi enim causam totius ordinis, suscepi causam rei publicae*); the passage says he took them up, and nothing either way about binding force.

So *suscipere*'s honest weakness for "binding acceptance" is that it names an undertaking that starts in the taker's own choice, not one owed to the one who offers. "Not binding" overreads L&S. For Lead L1, the first weakness should be read that way; the other weaknesses stand.

### 2. Additions: passages read during the forge and missing here

**Gaius, Institutes 3.102: the answer must match the question** [verified: thelatinlibrary.com/gaius3.html, §102, opened 2026-11-11]:

> *Adhuc inutilis est stipulatio, si quis ad id, quod interrogatus erit, non responderit, uelut si sestertia X a te dari stipuler, et tu nummum sestertium V milia promittas, aut si ego pure stipuler, tu sub condicione promittas.*
> "Further, a stipulation is void if someone does not answer to what he was asked: for example, if I stipulate for ten thousand sesterces to be given by you and you promise five thousand, or if I stipulate without condition and you promise under a condition." (translation mine)

*For the leads.* Lead L2's exchange holds only if the answer meets the question as asked. An answer that changes the sum or adds a condition leaves the stipulation void.

**Gaius 3.105: each party must hear the other** [verified: same page, §105]:

> *Mutum neque stipulari neque promittere posse palam est. idem etiam in surdo receptum est; quia et is, qui stipulatur, uerba promittentis, et qui promittit, uerba stipulantis exaudire debet.*
> "It is plain that a mute person can neither stipulate nor promise. The same has been accepted for a deaf person, because the one who stipulates must hear the words of the one who promises, and the one who promises must hear the words of the one who stipulates." (translation mine)

*For the leads.* Hearing is a condition of the pledge's validity, and so is speech: the section first excludes the mute. The hearing half is nearer the target's "hearing that completes itself in pledged uptake" than L2's "valid only if both speak".

Impetrabit te advocato atque arbitro. istac lege filiam tuam sponden mi uxorem dari? CH. Spondeo. C. Et ego spondeo idem hoc.*
  > "CHARM. I hear my daughter has been betrothed to you. LYS. Unless you are against it. CH. No, I am not against it. LYS. Then you pledge your daughter to me as wife? CHARM. I pledge, and a thousand gold Philippi as dowry. … LYS. He shall have it, with you as advocate and judge. On that condition, do you pledge that your daughter will be given me as wife? CH. I pledge. CALL. And I pledge the same." (translation mine)
- *Curculio* 671–675. Planesium calls the soldier Therapontigonus "my brother":
  > *Phaed. … Vt mihi hanc despondeas. Cvrc. Quid cessas, miles, hanc huic uxorem dare? T. Si haec volt. Pl. Mi frater, cupio. T. Fiat. C. Bene facis. Phaed. Spondesne, miles, mi hanc uxorem? Ther. Spondeo. Cvrc. Et ego hoc idem una spondeo.*
  > "PHAED. … That you betroth her to me. CURC. Why are you holding back, soldier, from giving her to him as wife? THER. If she wants it. PLAN. My brother, I want it. THER. So be it. CURC. Good. PHAED. Do you pledge her to me as wife, soldier? THER. I pledge. CURC. And I pledge the same along with him." (translation mine)

The forge's PRAGMA lens noted that only in the Curculio is the woman asked, and said that in every case "the speaker binds a dependant who does not speak". That holds for the Aulularia scene and both Trinummus scenes. It does not hold for Curculio: there her brother makes his consent depend on her wish (*Si haec volt*), and she answers in her own voice, *Mi frater, cupio*, though the *spondeo* is still his. In all four scenes the woman never says *spondeo*. In two (Trin. 1163, Curc. 675) a second man adds "I pledge the same"; I report the wording only and did not check what part such a second pledge plays.

*For the leads.* In these scenes *spondeo* binds, but what it pledges is a daughter or sister, and she never says it. Once it is given "since you want it so" (Trin. 573), and once the match is broken off (Aul. 783). These passages support "binds" in Lead L2. They do not support "free": the record's gloss of the donor as "the free answer that binds" (328-spondance.md, in its candidate table and under "Choices left to the owner") finds no support in them.
