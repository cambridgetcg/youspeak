# Kotowaru, enryo, jitai — "that I stubbornly did not accept"

_The accept dig's closest single-word gloss of binding acceptance was 承る uketamawaru, "receive-and-hear, and so consent". This file opens the Japanese words for the other answer. The native verb kotowaru first meant to divide a matter, to judge, to give reasons; it meant "to apologise" by the twelfth century; and the abridged Nihon kokugo daijiten's first citation for "to decline an offer" is Hepburn's dictionary of 1867. Japanese refuses, as Latin does, by giving reasons. Enryo, from the Analects' "far-reaching deliberation", became reserve toward others, then declining, and in Edo law a mild punishment of house confinement: the clearest case anywhere in this dig of a "polite refusal" that is in fact required. And the oldest Japanese tale, Taketori monogatari, gives the dig its best narrative witness. Kaguya-hime refuses five suitors by setting impossible tasks, and one of them dies of it. She refuses the emperor's summons to court outright, staking her life on it; the emperor answers with pressure on her father and an attempt to carry her off, which fails only because she can turn into a shadow. After that the refusal does not break the bond: they write to each other for three years, and her last letter, written as she leaves the world, names her refusal with the negative of the accept dig's own verb: 承らず, "I did not accept"._

## 1. The concept in compact form

**断る kotowaru.** The abridged Nihon kokugo daijiten (精選版 日本国語大辞典, on kotobank) glosses it as「こと（言・事）割る」の意, "to divide the word / the matter", and lists, with first citations [verified: kotobank.jp/word/断る-503348; translations mine]:
- ① to judge right and wrong; to decide a case (Nihon shoki, 720, Taika 1/8, in the Kitano-bon kun reading: 罪を判(コトワル), "judge crimes");
- ② to state the right of a matter, explain the principle (Tsurezuregusa, c. 1331, §141);
- ③ to understand (Genji monogatari, "Ukifune", c. 1001–14);
- ④ to explain reasons (Mōshi-shō, c. 1535); to notify in advance (jōruri, 1715); to report to an authority and get permission (Chōsokabe code, 1596); to annotate (1896);
- ⑤ **to give reasons and excuse oneself; to apologise** (Imakagami, 1170: ことはり申す限りなくて; 1900–01: 謝(コトハ)りなさい, "apologise!");
- ⑥ **to decline or refuse another's offer**: first citation Hepburn, *Wa-Ei gorin shūsei*, 1st ed. 1867: カネヲ カスコトヲ kotowaru, "to refuse to lend money";
- ⑦ to cut off a relation; to dismiss (Musui dokugen, 1843).

Daijisen adds that *kotowaru* is 「理る」と同語源, of one origin with *kotowari*, "reason, principle", and gives the modern senses: to notify in advance and get permission; "to tell the other side that one cannot comply with their offer; to refuse; to decline" (誘いを断る, "decline an invitation"); to say for safety's sake; **to break off a relation** (取り引きを断る, "end the dealings"). Its usage note: *kobamu* 拒む is to refuse "a coercive or compulsory demand", while *kotowaru* is the usual spoken word: せっかくだけど断るよ, "kind of you, but I'll decline", お断りします [verified: same page, デジタル大辞泉].

**遠慮 enryo.** NKD [verified: kotobank.jp/word/遠慮-38418; translations mine]:
- ① to look far ahead and think deeply (江吏部集, c. 1010–11; Genpei jōsuiki, 14th c.); the 字通 entry on the same page traces it to Lunyu 15.12, 人無遠慮，必有近憂;
- ② to hold back in words and actions toward others; to refrain out of consideration (Kyōgen *Kujizainin* in the 虎寛本 text, which NKD dates to the play's period, "late Muromachi to early modern" [the edition's romanization and own date not re-checked]: 遠慮なしにいふて見よ, "say it without reserve");
- ③ **to decline doing something; to withdraw from a place** (Ikaruga-no-shō hikitsuke, 1514; jōruri *Horikawa nami no tsuzumi*, c. 1706: 其座をゑんりょし立にけり, "she excused herself from the gathering and rose");
- ④ to refrain from celebration in sympathy with a bereavement;
- ⑤ **an Edo punishment for samurai and monks: confinement at home**, the gate shut but the side door left pulled to, and quiet comings and goings at night allowed (Tokugawa kinreikō, 1704);
- ⑥ a samurai's refraining from duty or audience when a death, an infectious illness or a criminal appeared in his family (1680).

Heibonsha's encyclopedia (改訂新版 世界大百科事典, by 平松義郎) on the same page: *enryo* was both a penalty and a self-imposed restraint; a samurai whose relative or retainer was punished "had to restrain himself voluntarily (*jihatsuteki ni*)", by first submitting a *sashihikae ukagai*, "a request whether to refrain", on whose direction duty-enryo or audience-enryo was ordered [verified: same page]. Daijisen's modern senses: restraint toward others (遠慮なくいただきます, "I'll help myself without reserve"); **declining; withdrawing** (せっかくですが出席を遠慮します, "kind of you, but I will refrain from attending"; and 君は遠慮してくれ, "would you kindly absent yourself"); deep deliberation as the original sense; the Edo penalty [verified].

**辞退 jitai.** NKD: 「へりくだる気持から命令や依頼などを受けないで引きさがること。自分にはふさわしくないとしてことわること。また単に、ことわること」, "to withdraw without accepting a command or request, out of a humble disposition; to decline something as unsuited to oneself; also simply to decline". First citations: Mutsu waki (late 11th c.?), a newly appointed governor who "heard the report of fighting, declined, and did not go to his post"; Heike monogatari (early 13th c.), book 3, the Tendai abbot "declining repeatedly"; Chinese source *Jinshu* [verified: kotobank.jp/word/辞退-520295].

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, or consciously decline:**

1. **Japanese refuses by giving reasons.** The native verb's history runs from dividing a matter and judging it, to explaining, to apologising, to declining (first cited 1867), to ending a relation. The decline grew out of the account.
2. **Enryo is the reserve that is expected, and was once imposed.** It is required restraint, a word for declining, the formula a host uses to dissolve it (遠慮なく, "without reserve"), and in Edo law a punishment one could be ordered to undergo, or ask permission to undergo.
3. **Jitai is the decline from humility, "as unsuited to oneself".** Formal and Sino-Japanese.
4. **The oldest tale holds both kinds of refusal.** Kaguya-hime's refusal by impossible condition (to the suitors) costs a life. Her direct refusal (to the emperor) is met first with pressure and an attempted abduction, and holds only because she is not of this world; after that it keeps the relation, and is named in her last letter with the negative of the accept verb, 承らず.

**Sibling distinctions.**
- **kotowaru** declines by explaining; it can also end a relation.
- **kobamu** resists a demand.
- **enryo** declines by holding back, and is expected to be pressed.
- **jitai** declines an honour or a post as unsuited to oneself.
- **uketamawarazu** is "not-accepting", the accept verb negated.

## 3. Primary attestations, each read to its end

### 3.1 Taketori monogatari — the suitors, the emperor, the letter

Text: Kokumin bunko edition on Japanese Wikisource, ruby markup removed by me [verified: ja.wikisource.org, 竹取物語 (國民文庫), raw wikitext; translations mine]. The date of composition is uncertain; the Wikisource versions page 竹取物語 (not the Kokumin bunko page's header, whose year field is empty) says it is held to have been composed between the latter 9th and the early 10th century [verified as stated there].

**(a) The old man asks; she answers with the accept verb.** The old man asks 「翁の申さんこと聞き給ひてんや。」, "will you listen to what I say?", and Kaguya-hime answers: 「何事をか宣はん事を承らざらん。」, "What could you say that I would not hear (承らざらん)?" He then urges her to marry [verified]. Because she is answering 聞く, the verb here is most simply the humble "hear" (NKD 承る ②), though NKD ③ "accept, consent" is possible; the line does not by itself show 承る meaning "accept" [verified: kotobank.jp/word/承る-438926, NKD senses ①–③].

**(b) The suitors: refusal by impossible condition.** She tells the old man to say to the five: 「ゆかしき物見せ給へらんに、御志勝りたり。とて仕うまつらん」, "whoever shows me the thing I wish to see, I will serve him, as the one whose devotion is greatest" [verified]. Each is set an impossible object. The Chūnagon Isonokami no Maro falls in his attempt and breaks his back; Kaguya sends him a poem of inquiry asking whether it is true, as she hears, that his waiting was in vain (まつかひなし, punning on *kai*, "shell" and "worth"); he writes a reply with difficulty and 「書きはてゝ絶え入り給ひぬ」, "finishing writing, he expired". 「これを聞きて、かぐや姫少し哀とおぼしけり」, "hearing this, Kaguya-hime felt a little pity" [verified]. The emperor later speaks of her as one who has "brought many men to nothing" (多くの人の身を徒になして) [verified].
- **Relation:** broken, and at a cost of life. **Not free, said plainly:** this refusal is disguised as a conditional acceptance. It never says no; it sets a price no one can pay. It is the opposite of the target: a no that pretends to be a yes.

**(c) The emperor's summons: a direct refusal.** The emperor sends a lady-in-waiting, who says that no one living in the world can fail to accept (承り) the king's command. Kaguya-hime: 「國王の仰事を背かばはや殺し給ひてよかし。」, "If I go against the king's command, let him kill me quickly." The emperor's answer: 「多くの人を殺してける心ぞかし。」, "that is a heart that has killed many", and he summons the old man and promises him rank [verified]. To the old man, who is promised rank if she serves: 「もはらさやうの宮仕つかう奉らじと思ふを、強ひて仕う奉らせ給はゞ消え失せなん。」, "I have no thought at all of serving at court like that; if you force me to serve, I shall vanish." [verified]

**(d) The bond kept after the refusal.** The emperor comes in person and moves to carry her off in his palanquin. She gives her ground, 「おのが身はこの國に生れて侍らばこそ仕へ給はめ」, "if I had been born in this land, I might serve you", and when he brings the palanquin she suddenly becomes a shadow (きと影になりぬ). He gives up, asks her to return to her form so that he may at least see her, and leaves with an exchange of poems, hers a refusal: 「葎はふ下にもとしは經ぬる身のなにかはたまのうてなをもみむ」, "I who have passed my years beneath the creeping weeds, why should I look upon a jewelled palace?" [verified]. Then: 「かぐや姫の御許にぞ御文を書きて通はさせ給ふ。御返事さすがに憎からず聞えかはし給ひて、おもしろき木草につけても、御歌を詠みてつかはす。…かやうにて、御心を互に慰め給ふほどに、三年ばかりありて」, "he sent letters to Kaguya-hime; her replies, after all, were not unkind, and they exchanged them, sending poems tied to lovely trees and grasses … and so, while they comforted each other's hearts, some three years passed" [verified].
- **Relation:** kept, and changed in kind. She refuses the form (service at court), and the relation continues in another (letters). **Not free, said plainly:** before the letters, the refusal is answered with pressure on her father and an attempted abduction, and it holds only because she can vanish; her ground is that she is not of this land. It is the dig's clearest narrative case of a refusal after which the table stays set, but the text does not show such a refusal being safe for a human refuser.

**(e) The last letter: 承らず.** As the heavenly beings come for her, Kaguya-hime stops them to write to the emperor, "very calmly, without haste":

> 「かく數多の人をたまひて留めさせ給へど、許さぬ迎まうできて、とり率て罷りぬれば、口をしく悲しきこと、宮仕つかう奉らずなりぬるも、かくわづらはしき身にて侍れば、心得ずおぼしめしつらめども、心強く承らずなりにしこと、なめげなるものに思し召し止められぬるなん、心にとまり侍りぬる。」
> 今はとて天のはごろもきるをりぞ君をあはれとおもひいでぬる
> "You sent so many men to keep me, but the escort that will not allow it has come to take me, and I go; it is a grief. That I did not serve at court was because I am so troublesome a being. You must have thought it past understanding, but that I stubbornly did not accept (心強く承らずなりにしこと), and that you should keep me in mind as someone rude: this stays in my heart." And the poem: "Now, as I put on the robe of heaven, I find I think of you with tenderness."
> [verified: same text; translation mine]

She sends the letter with the elixir of immortality. The emperor reads it, eats nothing, has no music, asks which mountain is nearest heaven, and writes: 「あふことも涙にうかぶわが身にはしなぬくすりも何にかはせむ」, "for me, afloat on tears that I shall never meet her, what use is a medicine against death?". He has a letter (御文; the text does not say whose, and it follows directly on his poem) attached to the jar of elixir she sent, and orders letter and jar set side by side on the summit of a mountain in Suruga and burned (御文・不死の藥の壺ならべて、火をつけてもやすべき). The envoy "accepted (承りて) that command", and the text derives the name: 兵士どもあまた具して山へ登りけるよりなん、その山をふしの山とは名づけゝる, "because he climbed the mountain with many soldiers, they named it *fushi no yama*". The text's own etymology is the soldiers, not the elixir of deathlessness (不死), whatever pun a reader hears. Its smoke still rises into the clouds [verified: same text, read to its end, 竹取物語　了].

What this gives:
- **Her refusal is named with the accept verb negated: 承らず.** The verb the accept dig found as the best Japanese gloss of binding acceptance (uptake-convergence-notes.md A6) appears here, in the oldest Japanese tale, as the word for the refusal that kept the bond. Here its object is a command (NKD ① "humbly receive a command" / ③ "consent"), unlike (a), where it answers "will you listen?".
- **The refusal is owned, not excused.** 心強く, "stubbornly, with a strong heart", and she does not withdraw it; she regrets only that he may think her rude (なめげ).
- **It ends with tenderness spoken to the refused one** (君をあはれと), as Elisha ends with "go in peace" and Xu You with "go home and rest".
- **But the text's word makes the no a not-yes.** The realm feature wants "a no that is a complete act and not the failure of a yes". Japanese, here, names this exemplary refusal by negating acceptance. That tension is the finding, and the forge must not hide it.

### 3.2 Enryo in use — the reserve to be dissolved, and the reserve to be served

- The override is attested from the Kyōgen stage: 遠慮なしにいふて見よ, "say it without reserve" (NKD ②, in the form 遠慮なしに; who speaks it to whom was not checked in the play) [verified as cited]. Modern 遠慮なくいただきます (Daijisen) is the guest's acceptance of that override [verified].
- 君は遠慮してくれ (Daijisen ②) uses the word for asking someone to leave: the polite form of exclusion [verified].
- The Edo penalty (NKD ⑤; Heibonsha; Britannica) and the self-request to refrain (*sashihikae ukagai*) show the word carrying a restraint one could be ordered into, or be expected to ask for [verified].

**Not free, said plainly:** *enryo* is the textbook case of a decline that is expected, often meant to be overcome, and sometimes imposed. Of the Japanese words it is the furthest from a sovereign refusal.

## 4. Etymology and family

- **kotowaru** < *koto* (言・事, "word; matter") + *waru* (割る, "divide, split") (NKD) [verified as stated there]. The decline is the last of the verb's many senses to be cited.
- **enryo** < Chinese 遠慮, "far deliberation" (字通 cites 論語・衛霊公 without a section number; the line is 15.11 or 15.12 by edition, archaeology/chinese/ci-rang-declining.md §3.5) [verified]. NKD's first citation for the "decline" sense is 1514 [verified]; that the sense is a Japanese development, absent in Chinese, is my inference and was not checked.
- **jitai** < Chinese 辭退 (*Jinshu*, via NKD) [verified]; 辭 is the subject of archaeology/chinese/ci-rang-declining.md.
- **uketamawaru** < *uke(ru)* "receive" + *tamawaru* "be granted" (uptake-convergence-notes.md, verified there). Its kanji 承 and the 受 of *ukeru* are Chinese contact.
- **Doctrinal family.** *kotowaru* (decline by reasons) · *kotowari* (reason) · *kobamu* (resist) · *enryo* (reserve) · *jitai* (humble decline) · *yuzuru* (yield) · *uketamawaru / uketamawarazu* (accept / not accept).
- **Pairing with acceptance.** In Taketori: 承らざらん (to the old man, answering "will you listen?") and 承らず (of the emperor's command), the same verb, positive and negated, though probably not in the same sense (§3.1(a)) [verified]. The envoy's 承りて at the end is the plain acceptance of a command [verified]. In modern usage *kotowaru* is paired with *hikiukeru* 引き受ける "take on" and *jitai* with *judaku* 受諾 "acceptance" [from memory: not re-checked].

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

- Saying no to the form of a relation (service at court) while keeping the relation itself (letters, three years).
- Owning a refusal as one's own ("stubbornly") and still speaking tenderly to the one refused.
- The difference between declining by explanation (*kotowaru*), by reserve (*enryo*) and by humility (*jitai*), which Japanese keeps apart and English collapses into "decline".

**Where the polite refusal is not free, said plainly:** *enryo* is expected and dissolved by the other (遠慮なく), and in Edo law could be ordered; *jitai* is framed as humility; and Kaguya-hime's refusal of the suitors by impossible condition is a no that wears the clothes of a yes, and kills.

Near-misses:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| *kotowaru* | the everyday verb, which also means "end the dealings"; declines by explaining |
| *enryo* | expected restraint, to be overridden; an old penalty |
| *jitai* | humble and formal; Sino-Japanese |
| *uketamawarazu* | the accept verb negated: the failure of a yes |
| English *decline* | flattens the three Japanese kinds into one |

**Canon collision check of the region:** the canon's Japanese-donor words (satorime, kenshome, daigome, shinjinme, tarikiqing, kintsugime, jiritsume, and the *ki* and *ma* words kimme, kimance and panimaance) do not touch declining. **warame** (abstention, Arabic) and **walkekin** (bond surviving silence) are the semantic neighbours across the canon (archaeology/hebrew/meun-refusal.md §5).

## 6. Convergence-mapping

Japonic share of the cross-family picture:
1. **Decline lexicalised as reason-giving.** *kotowaru* (divide the matter → explain → apologise → decline). Compare Latin *ex-/re-cusare* (< *causa*), Arabic *iʿtadhara ʿan*, Chinese 辭 (plea, words). Native Japonic, so independent by descent; possibly typologically common [my inference].
2. **Decline written as not-accepting.** 承らず. Compare 辤 = 不受 (Chinese) and *lā qabbəlītū* (Aramaic, b. Shabbat 88a).
3. **The ritual refusal designed to be overridden.** *enryo* and 遠慮なく. Sino-Japanese, so in contact with Chinese 禮辭 / 固辭; compare the Hebrew and Latin cases.
4. **A refusal that keeps the relation and changes its form.** Kaguya-hime and the emperor. Compare m. Yevamot 13:4 (Hebrew), Q 33:72 (Arabic), Mengzi 2B3 (Chinese).
5. **The refusal disguised as a condition, which harms.** The suitors' tasks. No close parallel was found in the other digs.

Independence: *kotowaru* and the Taketori narrative are Japonic; *enryo*, *jitai* and the kanji of 承 are contact with Sinitic. For the strict count, Japanese adds an independent witness on shapes 1 and 4, and a contact witness on shape 3.

## 7. Connectable canon-words

- **jiritsume** (Core; Japanese 自律, self-regulation): the nearest Japanese-donor word to "sovereign"; it names self-attuned cadence, not refusal.
- **walkekin** (Core): Kaguya-hime and the emperor are a refusal walkekin would recognise: a bond carried by letters across a no.
- **warame** (Core): restraint as a disposition; *enryo* is its social, enforced cousin.
- The accept dig's lead A6 (承る uketamawaru, uptake-convergence-notes.md §5): Taketori shows the verb's negation as the decline.

## 8. Forge-targets identified — leads only

No lemma, no coinage, no score.

- **Lead J-D1 — kotowaru / kotowari.**
  - Carries: the native Japanese decline, grown from "divide the matter, give the reason"; kin to *kotowari*, "reason".
  - Weaknesses: the everyday modern verb, so a coinage would read as a loan of a common word; it also means "break off a relation" (sense ⑦; Daijisen 4), which is the opposite of the target; it declines by explaining, not by sovereign act; four morae; *koto* already appears in canon bodies as *kotodama* (§9).
- **Lead J-D2 — enryo.**
  - Carries: the decline that keeps harmony by holding back.
  - Weaknesses: the clearest "not free" refusal of the dig; an Edo punishment; expected to be overridden; Sino-Japanese.
- **Lead J-D3 — uketamawarazu (承らず).**
  - Carries: the decline named in the oldest tale's best refusal, and the morphological twin of the accept dig's lead A6, so the two performatives would share a stem.
  - Weaknesses: it makes the decline the negation of the accept, exactly what the realm feature excludes ("not the failure of a yes"); seven morae; humble keigo, rank-coded; its inner syllables reproduce the stem of the Core word warame (§9).
- **Lead J-D4 — jitai.**
  - Carries: declining an honour or post "as unsuited to oneself"; "not mine".
  - Weaknesses: framed as humility; formal; Sino-Japanese (Chinese 辭退); common Japanese homophones (字体 "typeface", 事態 "situation", 自体 "itself") [from memory: not re-checked]; the *ji-* onset is crowded in canon (§9).
- **Lead J-D5 — yuzuru (譲る).**
  - Carries: native "hand over, yield", the Japanese reading of 讓.
  - Weaknesses: attested here only through Unihan's kun reading; not dug; it means yielding what is one's own, not declining what is offered.
