# yumi — the we that includes you, in Tok Pisin's own paradigm

_The first Tok Pisin file (yumi-and-clusivity.md) named the donor and cited nothing. This file opens the
whole first-person system the donor belongs to — mi, yu, em; mipela against yumi; the dual and trial
forms built with tu and tri; the formative -pela — and follows it back to Samoan plantations, to the
Tolai pronouns it was patterned on, and forward into Bible translation, newspapers and children's
speech. Two findings matter most for the anchor chapter. First, yumi is the inclusive plural, not the
dyad: "you and I" is yumitupela. Second, the visible yu + mi inside yumi is not an Oceanic inheritance
— the Tolai inclusive is opaque — but what happens when a substrate category is spelled out in
lexifier words. The transparency is the pidgin's own creation._

## 1. The concept in compact form

Tok Pisin pronouns mark person, number (singular, dual, trial, plural) and, in the first person
non-singular, clusivity: whether the addressee is inside the "we". Mühlhäusler's table in the
*Handbook of Tok Pisin* gives the paradigm as follows [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, "Syntax of Tok
Pisin", Handbook C-70, p. 343]:

| | 1st inclusive | 1st exclusive | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| singular | — | **mi** [mi] "I" | **yu** [ju] "you (one)" | **em** [em] "he, she, it" |
| dual | **yumitupela** [ˈjumitupela] "we two (incl.)" | **mitupela** [ˈmitupela] "we two (excl.)" | **yutupela** [ˈjutupela] "you two" | **(em) tupela** [ˈtupela] "they two" |
| trial | **yumitripela** [ˈjumitɾipela] "we three (incl.)" | **mitripela** [ˈmitɾipela] "we three (excl.)" | **yutripela** [ˈjutɾipela] "you three" | **(em) tripela** [ˈtɾipela] "they three" |
| plural | **yumi** [ˈjumi] "we (all) (incl.)" | **mipela** [ˈmipela] "we (all) (excl.)" | **yupela** [ˈjupela] "you (all)" | **(em) ol** [ol] "they" |

IPA in this file is a broad transcription by the excavator on Tok Pisin's five-vowel system, with
⟨r⟩ as the usual tap [ɾ] [verified: Wikipedia "Tok Pisin", Phonology, citing Smith 2008, pp. 195–200];
word-initial stress is [from memory: not re-checked]. Mühlhäusler introduces the table: "Tok Pisin
distinguishes essentially seven pronouns, i.e. three for the singular and four for the plural; to this
dual and trial forms must be added" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 343]. Wikipedia adds that "The
paradigm varies depending on the local languages; dual number is common, while the trial is less so",
and its table, citing Verhaar (1995: 354), gives "the largest Tok Pisin pronoun inventory" with *yumipela
or yumi* for the inclusive plural [verified: Wikipedia "Tok Pisin", Grammar; Verhaar not opened].

**Structural properties the forge must preserve, if it uses this donor at all:**

1. **Clusivity lives only in the first person non-singular.** There is no inclusive singular; the
   inclusive/exclusive split appears in dual, trial and plural alike [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p.
   343].
2. **yumi is the inclusive plural, not the dyad.** Mühlhäusler glosses *yumi* "we (all) (incl.)" and
   *yumitupela* "we two (incl.)" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 343]; Mosel's comparative table has
   the same split [verified: Mosel 1980, *Tolai and Tok Pisin*, Pacific Linguistics B-73, p. 60]. In
   running text *yumi* includes groups: the shepherds of Luke 2 say to one another "yumi go long
   Betlehem" (§3.1). The dyad "you and I" is *yumitupela*, colloquially reduced to /yumitla/ (§4.2).
3. **Every non-singular form is visibly built from English words**: person root (*mi*, *yu*, *yu+mi*),
   optional numeral (*tu* "two", *tri* "three"), and the formative *-pela* "fellow". That transparency
   is the property the anchor chapter's Law of Visible Constituents rests on, and §4.3 shows where it
   comes from.
4. **-pela pluralises only mi and yu.** "- pela can only be added to the first and second person
   singular pronouns": *mi → mipela*, *yu → yupela*, but *em → (em) ol*, and the inclusive is *yumi*,
   not a *-pela* form [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985b, "Inflectional morphology of Tok Pisin", Handbook
   C-70, p. 336]. Mühlhäusler asks why *mipela* "and not yumi should be regarded as the 'plural' of
   mi" [verified: same, pp. 336–337] — the inclusive is a person category of its own, not a plural of I.

The etymon column is the standard etymology for every form; for *yumi* the "(and)" is APiCS's
bracket, not an attested English phrase. I found no source in this session that dates the first
written *yumi*; see §4.2.

**Sibling distinctions — four words, four standing-places.** *yumi* puts the addressee inside the
group and leaves the group's size open. *mipela* puts the speaker inside and the addressee outside.
*yumitupela* is exactly two, speaker and addressee. *ol* is the group the speaker stands outside. The
possessives keep the same split: *bilong mipela* "our (excl.)", *bilong yumi* "our (incl.)" [verified:
Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 356].

## 3. Primary attestations and scholarly references

### 3.1 yumi and mipela in real texts

- **The missionary's rule.** Mühlhäusler quotes Laycock: "The distinction of 'inclusive' and
  'exclusive' is a feature taken over from Melanesian languages in New Guinea. One must always
  distinguish in Pidgin whether 'we' includes the person or persons spoken to or not. If the addressee
  is not included, one says mipela; if he is, one says yumi." A missionary must say *Jisas i-dai long
  yumi* "Jesus died for us", "that is, for Europeans and natives alike"; *Jisas i-dai long mipela* "would
  mean Jesus died for us (missionaries) (and not for the congregation)" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c,
  p. 343, quoting Laycock 1970c: xviii; Laycock not opened; hyphen as printed].
- **Narrator to reader.** Matthew 1:23: "As bilong dispela nem i olsem, God i stap wantaim yumi" — the
  meaning of this name is: God is with us [verified: Buk Baibel long Tok Pisin, Matyu 1:23,
  https://ebible.org/tpi/MAT01.htm; © 1969–2008 The Bible Society of Papua New Guinea].
- **A group, not a dyad.** Luke 2:15, the shepherds to one another: "Goan, yumi go long Betlehem na yumi
  lukim dispela samting i kamap, em Bikpela i bin tokim yumi long en" [verified: Buk Baibel, Luk 2:15,
  https://ebible.org/tpi/LUK02.htm]. *yumi* here is the whole band of shepherds.
- **Prayer to God takes the exclusive.** Matthew 6:9–13: "Papa bilong mipela, yu stap long heven …
  Nau yu ken givim mipela kaikai inap long dispela de. Na yu ken lusim ol rong bilong mipela, olsem
  mipela i save lusim ol rong ol arapela i mekim long mipela …" [verified: Buk Baibel, Matyu 6:9–13,
  https://ebible.org/tpi/MAT06.htm]. The praying community does not include its addressee, God, in its
  "we", so the grammar requires *mipela* throughout. This bears directly on the chapter's vocative
  example (see §8, lead 3).
- **The national register.** "yumi mas gohetim kantri bilong yumi" — we must make our country advance
  [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 361]; "Yumi mas rausim pasin nogut olsem baimbai kantri bilong yumi i
  ken gohet" — we have to get rid of undesirable customs so that our country can progress [verified:
  same, p. 407]. Mühlhäusler quotes written Tok Pisin "from *Nius bilong Yumi*, 15 October 1972", a
  periodical whose own title uses *yumi* [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 140]; that it was a newspaper
  is [from memory: not re-checked].
- **Hortative.** "Orait, yumi i go nau!" — O.K., let's go! [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 364]. The
  earliest grammar already has "i gut yumi go" — it is good for us to go, let us go [verified:
  Mühlhäusler 1985a, pp. 94 and 117, citing Brenninkmeyer 1924: 23–24; Brenninkmeyer not opened].

**What complicates the tidy picture.** Read to their ends, the witnesses show the distinction is real
but not uniform:

- Mühlhäusler: "a confusion of inclusive and exclusive pronouns is often found in speakers for whom this
  distinction is not made in their first language", and the newer *yumipela* "for some of its users
  … corresponds to English we" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 343]. In a conversation recorded
  among students of the University of Papua New Guinea in 1976, which Mühlhäusler gives as an example of
  anglicised, code-switching Tok Pisin, the answer to "you and who else?" is "Charles, Isidor, yumipela
  na wantaim tambu bilong em" — glossed "Us, Charles, Isidor and his cousin" — though the questioner was
  not at the party [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, pp. 132–133; that the questioner is excluded is my
  reading of the dialogue]. The witness is therefore one register (anglicised student speech), not
  Tok Pisin at large.
- A list member writing to the Mihalic Project list in 2000 reported that in Enga and Ipili country,
  whose languages have no clusivity, Tok Pisin *mipela* is used for "we two" and *yumi* for "we more
  than two", following the vernacular dual/plural [verified:
  https://mailman.anu.edu.au/pipermail/mihalic/2000-November/000237.html; informal witness, not
  peer-reviewed].
- A Tok Pisin rendering of Article 1 of the Universal Declaration found on the Internet Archive opens
  "Yumi olgeta mama karim umi long stap fri …" and then continues "… na mipela olgeta I mas mekim
  gutpela pasin long ol narapela" — inclusive and exclusive inside one article addressed to everyone,
  and reduced spellings *umi* and *Uumi* [verified:
  https://archive.org/download/UniversalDeclarationOfHumanRightsTokPisin/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights_Tok_Pisin.pdf,
  Article 1]. The PDF ends with the copyright line of the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human
  Rights (OHCHR-UNOG), so it is a copy of OHCHR's published Tok Pisin text, not an anonymous found text;
  the translator is not named, and the original on ohchr.org was not opened.

### 3.2 Scholarly afterlife

- **Mosel (1980)**, *Tolai and Tok Pisin: The Influence of the Substratum on the Development of New
  Guinea Pidgin*, compares Tok Pisin with Samoan Plantation Pidgin, Bislama, Cape York Creole and five
  Patpatar–Tolai languages and concludes: "The personal pronoun system of Tok Pisin is evidently
  patterned after those of the substratum languages" [verified: p. 60, §4.4]. Her sentence "The use of
  the suffix -pela does not go back to Tolai substratum influence" stands on the same page but closes
  her §4.3 on **adjectives**, so it is said of adjectival *-pela*, not of the pronouns [verified: p. 60];
  it should not be quoted as a statement about *mipela* or *yupela*.
- **Mühlhäusler (1985)** is more cautious about substrates in general: "In the past the role of
  transfers has been widely overrated", and substratum influence "makes itself felt late rather than
  early" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 77]. Of the pronouns specifically he says the early stable
  system is one where "the Melanesian way of pronoun-reference has replaced the earlier English system"
  [verified: p. 96]. The two accounts agree on the pronouns and differ on substrate influence elsewhere.
- **Mosel (1980: 1–2)** records the older debate: Wurm (1971a) credited Tolai with "a considerable
  number of lexical elements" and "some structural characteristics", Fry (1977) judged that Kuanua "has
  probably had more influence on Pidgin than any other single New Guinea language";
  Mühlhäusler (1975a) answered that "the importance of speakers of Tolai in the formation of NGP is
  also debatable" [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 1, quoting Mühlhäusler 1975a: 3].
- **Keesing (1988)**, *Melanesian Pidgin and the Oceanic Substrate*, argues that Melanesian Pidgin
  grammar, pronouns included, replicates an Eastern Oceanic substrate pattern laid down largely in
  nineteenth-century Pacific maritime contact [from memory: not re-checked; the book was not opened].
  APiCS calls the Melanesian case "quite well-known (see, e.g., Keesing 1988, Siegel 2008)" [verified:
  APiCS ch. 15]: one of two example references, not "the standard reference". A review in *Pacific
  Studies* was found by search but the site refused connection, so its contents are not reported here.
- **APiCS (2013).** Haspelmath and Michaelis list Tok Pisin *yumi*/*mipela*, Bislama *yumi*/*mifala* and
  Kriol *yunmi, minyu*/*minbala, mindubala* as cases where "the presence of the distinction is evidently
  due to Austronesian influence", and conclude that, given the dual and trial forms, "it has long been
  clear that the pattern must have been created on the basis of the substrates" [verified:
  https://apics-online.info/parameters/15.chapter.html]. Two misprints there must not be copied: the
  chapter text says "The exclusive form is yumi" where its own table gives *yumi* as inclusive; and the
  Tok Pisin datapoint for the dual gives *mipela* as "1du.excl" where every grammar has *mitupela*
  [verified: https://apics-online.info/valuesets/22-14].
- **Mihalic (1971)**, *The Jacaranda Dictionary and Grammar of Melanesian Pidgin*, is the standard
  dictionary and the bearer of the standard spelling (§4.4). Its entries for *yumi* and *mipela* were not
  opened in this session: no free copy was found.
- **Verhaar (1995)**, *Toward a Reference Grammar of Tok Pisin*, a corpus grammar built largely on Bible
  and newspaper text, is cited by Wikipedia for the full inventory with *yumipela* (p. 354) [verified as
  Wikipedia's citation only; Verhaar not opened].
- **Smith (2002)**, *Growing Up with Tok Pisin*, documents first-language Tok Pisin among young speakers
  [from memory: not re-checked; not opened]. That its corpus is archived at PARADISEC as collection
  GS1 is [from memory: not re-checked — a search-result snippet only; the catalogue page refused the
  request (HTTP 403) on re-check].

The articulation this file receives: the categories (clusivity, dual, trial) are Oceanic; the words are
English; the assembly, and with it the visible yu + mi, is Tok Pisin's.

## 4. Etymology and family

### 4.1 The English lexifier

*mi* < *me*, *yu* < *you*, *em* < *him*, *ol* < *all*, *-pela* < *fellow*, *tu* < *two*, *tri* < *three*
[verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, pp. 92–93 for *-pela/-fela* "from fellow" and *-im* "from him"; Lund Language
Diversity Forum blog, O. Lundgren, 17 September 2021, for the numerals and *ol*,
https://lldf.blogg.lu.se/you-me-fellow-and-me-three-fellow-on-tok-pisin-personal-pronouns].
English supplied object forms (*me*, *him*), not subject forms; the early stable system lost the
subject/object distinction except in the third singular, *hi* against *him/em* [verified: Mühlhäusler
1985a, p. 96, citing Thurnwald 1913: 97 and Brenninkmeyer 1924: 12].

### 4.2 History of the forms

1. **Jargon stage (Pacific contact, to the 1880s).** No consistent singular/plural or first/second
   person distinction; "in giving commands, English we frequently refers to second person singular and
   plural" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 83].
2. **Samoan Plantation Pidgin (SPP).** A stabilised pidgin existed in Samoa by 1870, close to the
   Bichelamar of the New Hebrides; after 1879 the plantation labour came only from the Bismarck
   Archipelago — about 6,000 workers by 1914, over half from New Ireland and about a fifth from the
   Gazelle Peninsula, the Tolai homeland, and none from the mainland [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 2, citing
   Mühlhäusler 1975a: 4f., 15]. SPP already distinguished *yumi* (1pl incl.) from *miol* (1pl excl.),
   with *yuol*, *emol*, and had no dual or trial [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 60]. The first stabilisation
   "occurred among the ethnically and linguistically diverse plantation workers on the Samoan
   plantations of the Deutsche Handels- und Plantagengesellschaft" [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 90].
3. **Stabilised Tok Pisin (after 1900).** The pronoun system "which has remained the 'standard' until
   today": *mi, yu, em, mipela, yumi, yupela, (em) ol* [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 96]. Two
   pluralising strategies had competed — *-pela* (*mipela, yupela, himpela*) and following *ol* (*mi ol,
   yu ol, him ol*); *em ol* "is still a reflection" of the second [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985b, p. 337].
   *empela* is rare [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 93].
4. **Dual and trial.** Present in the modern paradigm but absent from SPP [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 60].
   When they entered was not established from the sources opened.
5. **Reanalysis among first-language speakers.** Lynch (1979) reports reduced forms /yumitla/, /mitla/,
   /yutla/, /tupla/, /mipla/, /yupla/; the dual reductions invite a new analysis, root + *-t-* "dual" or
   *-p-* "plural" + *-la* [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985b, p. 337, citing Lynch 1979; Lynch not opened].
6. **yumipela.** A newer general "we" for some speakers (§2, §3.1).

First attestation of *yumi*: not established. A search result described a review of Keesing (1988) as
reproducing his dated table of first attestations, but the review could not be opened and the snippet
was garbled, so no date is given here.

### 4.3 The Oceanic substrate — and why it is not the source of the transparency

Mosel sets the Tok Pisin system beside five Patpatar–Tolai languages [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 61]. The
Tolai column (broad IPA by the excavator; Tolai ⟨v⟩ as [β] is [from memory: not re-checked]):

| | 1 incl. | 1 excl. | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sg | — | iau [iau] | u [u] | ia [ia] |
| du | dor [dor] | amir [amir] | amur [amur] | dir [dir] |
| tr | datal [datal] | amital [amital] | amutal [amutal] | dital [dital] |
| pl | dat [dat] | avet [aβet] | avat [aβat] | diat [diat] |

The same forms, with glosses "thou and I", "both of you, and I", "all of you, and I", appear in
Wikipedia's article, which notes that plural pronouns lose final *-t* before a verb (*da vana* "let's
go!") [verified: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolai_language; uncited there, but it matches Mosel's
table]. Mosel adds that these pronouns "show some relation to the numerals denoting 'two', 'three' and
'four', though a clear-cut segmentation as in Tok Pisin is not possible" [verified: Mosel 1980, p. 61].

What the table shows, read plainly: the Tolai inclusive is **not** built from *u* "you" and *iau* "I".
*dor*, *datal*, *dat* share their *d-* with the third-person non-singulars *dir*, *dital*, *diat*; the
exclusive *amir* differs from second-person *amur* by one vowel. (These resemblances are my
observation from the table, not a claim of any source.) Across Austronesian the inclusive and exclusive
are, in Adelaar and Hajek's words, "lexemes sui generis … not derived from one another nor from 1SG or
2SG pronouns" [verified: Adelaar & Hajek, "Free personal pronouns", preprint of ch. 52 of *The Oxford
Guide to the Malayo-Polynesian Languages of Southeast Asia*, p. 3,
https://rest.mars-prod.its.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/c41b7c9d-149f-4cdb-803a-715211f8479e/content].

So the substrate supplied the **category** — a we that must say whether you are in it, in four numbers —
and an opaque set of forms. The plantation workers filled that category with the English words they
had: *you* + *me*, *two*, *three*, *fellow*. The visible constituents of *yumi* are an achievement of
pidgin formation, the same achievement visible in Bislama *yumi* and Kriol *yunmi* and *minyu* [verified:
APiCS ch. 15, table 2]. Chinese Pidgin English is only a distant parallel: APiCS codes it "No
inclusive/exclusive distinction", and English *we* is rendered "variously as my, my long you or two
man" [verified: APiCS datapoint 20-15, Li & Matthews, https://apics-online.info/valuesets/20-15], so
*my long you* "me with you" is an occasional phrase, not an inclusive pronoun. None of these is an
independent witness to Tok Pisin; they share a lexifier and, apart from Chinese Pidgin English, a
Pacific lineage.

### 4.4 Orthography and pronunciation

- Until missionaries in German New Guinea took up orthography "after the turn of the century", Tok
  Pisin words were spelled after their English sources, with errors such as *he* for the predicate
  marker *i*, which Wurm calls "of Austronesian origin" [verified: Wurm 1985, p. 167]. (Wikipedia's
  "Tok Pisin" article dates this etymological spelling to before 1955 while citing Wurm; Wurm's own
  text does not say that: pp. 169–170 describe a Catholic (SVD) mission spelling devised "well before
  World War I" and many competing systems, "amongst them nine major systems", before the 1955 reform.)
- In 1955 the Territory administration moved to standardise; T. Dietz and L. Luzbetak devised an
  orthography based on the north-coast (Madang) pronunciation, decreed by the Department of Education
  (1956) and used, with minor changes, in Mihalic's grammar and dictionary of 1957 [verified: Wurm 1985,
  pp. 170–171].
- A Lutheran-modified spelling, pressed for by indigenous speakers literate in Tok Pisin, was used in the New
  Testament of 1968 (40,000 copies sold in nine months); in 1969 an Orthography Committee chaired by
  F. Mihalic and J. Sievert recognised the New Testament spelling as the norm, and Mihalic's 1971
  edition (the Jacaranda dictionary) "employed" it [verified: Wurm 1985, pp. 173–175].
- Pronoun spellings in that standard: *mi, yu, em, yumi, mipela, yupela, ol, yumitupela, mitupela*
  [verified: as printed in Mühlhäusler 1985c, p. 343]. Speech and much informal writing reduce *-pela* to
  *-pla*, "more so for pronouns" [verified: Wikipedia "Tok Pisin", Grammar; APiCS 22-15 *mipela/mipla*].
- Sounds: five vowels; ⟨y⟩ = [j]; ⟨r⟩ usually a tap [ɾ] [verified: Wikipedia "Tok Pisin", Phonology,
  citing Smith 2008]. In older varieties stress contrasts were slight (Wurm 1977d: 513), and reduction of
  unstressed syllables in grammatical words is recent [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985a, p. 109]. The loss of
  the second vowel in /mipla/ and /tupla/ implies that it is unstressed [verified: Mühlhäusler 1985b,
  p. 337, "loss of the unstressed vowel"]; initial stress in *yumi* [ˈjumi] is [from memory: not
  re-checked].

### 4.5 Family and contact

Tok Pisin, Bislama (Vanuatu) and Solomons Pijin are sister varieties of Melanesian Pidgin; Bislama has
*yumi* against *mifala* [verified: APiCS ch. 15, table 2]; Solomons Pijin *iumi* [ˈiumi] is [from memory:
not re-checked]. Australian Kriol has *yunmi* [ˈjunmi] and *minyu* [ˈminju] for the inclusive [verified:
APiCS ch. 15, table 2; IPA by the excavator]. For convergence counts these Melanesian Pidgin varieties
and Kriol are one witness. Norf'k (Pitcairn–Norfolk, a separate English-based lineage) has a dual
inclusive *himii/hamii* "corresponding to the Melanesian form yumi", but APiCS files it under
distinctions "not (or not clearly) derived from a substrate", says the distinction exists only in the
dual, and finds it "somewhat questionable" whether the exclusive forms *miienhem, miienher* are fully
grammaticalized [verified: APiCS ch. 15, §4]. *himii* does not visibly contain *yu*, so it is no
witness for visible constituents.

## 5. The unnamed region — the gap-shadows

English *we* does not say whether the hearer is in it. Everyday cases where that silence is felt:

- "We need to talk" from a manager: is it the manager and you, or the manager and the board, about you?
- "We're leaving at six" said at a party: an invitation, or a goodbye?
- "Should we tell them?" — which side of the secret is the hearer on?

Near-misses, each failing:

| Candidate | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| English *you and I* | marks the dyad only, and only by spelling both out; has no plural inclusive |
| English *let's* | inclusive in force but only for proposals, never for statements [from memory: not re-checked] |
| English *all of us* / *the rest of us* | adds scope words around an unmarked *we*; the hearer's place is still inferred |
| Quechua *ñuqanchik*, Malay *kita*, Mandarin *咱們* | mark inclusion, but none keeps "you" visible (see the companion survey summarised in §6) |

**Canon-collision check (2026-11-10, from the repo's canon/ and the chapter):** *synophora* names a
shared witnessing, not who is inside a we; *autoxenia* names I-am-you, which the chapter says *yumi*
supersedes by keeping both persons distinct; *ubuntume* (Core) names personhood through community;
*mitakuyame* (Core) names kinship with all beings. None names "a we that includes the hearer"; the
anchor particles are grammatical inventory rather than canon words, as the chapter itself says.

## 6. Convergence-mapping

**Clusivity itself** is common and independently attested: WALS codes 63 of 200 sampled languages with
inclusive and exclusive differentiated in independent pronouns and 5 more with only the inclusive
differentiated [verified: Cysouw, WALS ch. 39, https://wals.info/chapter/39]. Its first European
description is Domingo de Santo Tomás's Quechua grammar of 1560 [verified: same].

**Visible you + I inside the inclusive**, the property the Law of Visible Constituents needs, is rare.
The companion survey (archaeology/tok-pisin/clusivity-survey.md) of eight languages (Quechua, Cantonese,
Mandarin, Malay, Tamil, Hawaiian, Ojibwe, Cherokee) found none whose inclusive is built from its own
"you" and "I" words. Ojibwe comes nearest: *giinawind* "we (including you)" carries the stem of *giin*
"you" plus the first-plural ending of *niinawind* "we (excluding you)" [verified: Ojibwe People's
Dictionary entries; UW Ojibwe grammar paradigm, which labels the inclusive "21"]. Fully visible
compositions come from contact languages (this file, §4.3) and from phrase-level conjunction in a
language without a 'we', as in Pirahã *ti gíxai* "I you" [verified: WALS ch. 39, ex. 1, citing Everett
1986: 281].

Witness count for "an inclusive we that keeps both persons visible": **1 contact cluster** (English-
lexified Pacific pidgins and creoles, Tok Pisin among them), **plus a partial Algonquian pattern**
(you-stem + we-ending), **plus phrase-level conjunctions**. Cantonese 我哋 is not a witness: it keeps
only 我 "I", and "There is no inclusive/exclusive 'we' in Cantonese" [verified: APiCS 20-15, comment by
Li & Matthews, https://apics-online.info/valuesets/20-15].

Also checked: the first file's claim that "grammars that mark how-you-know tend also to mark
whom-you-include". In the 191 languages coded in both WALS 39A and 77A, 29 of 64 languages with
clusivity (45%) have grammatical evidentials, and so do 57 of 127 without it (45%) [verified: computed
by the excavator from https://wals.info/feature/39A.tab and https://wals.info/feature/77A.tab,
counting "Only inclusive" with clusivity]. The sample shows no association.

## 8. Leads (excavation only — nothing here is coined, scored or forged)

1. **Lead: a dual anchor.** If the chapter wants an anchor for exactly two persons, the donor's own form
   is *yumitupela*, reduced in speech to /yumitla/. *Weaknesses:* four or five syllables against one
   breath (EUMATHE); *-pela* carries English *fellow*; a second inclusive anchor competes with *yumi:*
   and needs the language owner's decision on what *yumi:* means.
2. **Lead: re-ground the Law of Visible Constituents.** The law can stand on Tok Pisin *yumi* (with its
   sister creoles counted once) and on the Algonquian "21" pattern, and can drop 我哋. *Weaknesses:* the
   evidence becomes "mostly contact languages", which is honest but less universal than the chapter's
   tone; Ojibwe and Cree are living Indigenous languages and would need their own protocol file before
   being cited as donors.
3. **Lead: prayer register.** The donor's community, praying to God, says *mipela* (Matthew 6). An
   anchor for "we, speaking together to You" would, by the donor's grammar, be exclusive.
   *Weaknesses:* the chapter's Inclusive Blessing withholds anchors from the exclusive; adopting this
   lead means amending a law, which is the language owner's call, not an excavator's.
